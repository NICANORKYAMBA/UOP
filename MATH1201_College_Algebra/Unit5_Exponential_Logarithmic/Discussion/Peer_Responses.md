## Peer Response 1: to Delissa Jonsson

Hi Delissa,

Your step-by-step conversion into exponential form is very clear, and your shortcut, 7.7 - 5.7 = 2
so 10² = 100, is the neatest way to see the comparison. Your Mendocino example also shows
something interesting: a 5.6 earthquake with a reading of only about 398 mm still caused injuries
and power cuts. That is because magnitude measures the size of the earthquake at its source, while
the damage depends on depth, distance, and the buildings nearby (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.). It
also helps to know that each whole step in magnitude means 10 times the wave amplitude but about 32
times the energy released, so the 7.7 event released roughly 1,000 times more energy than a 5.7.
How do you think a shallow quake like Mendocino compares with a deeper one of the same magnitude?

Best,

## Peer Response 3: to Mustafa Mohamed

Hi Mustafa,

I like that you used the Sudan earthquake, and your reading of 39.81 mm for magnitude 4.6 is
correct. I think a small slip crept into your first two answers, though. 0.001 × 10^7.7 equals
10^4.7, which is about 50,118.72 mm, not 5,011.87, and 0.001 × 10^5.7 equals 10^2.7, about
501.19 mm. Both of your values are 10 times too small, which is why your ratio still came out right
at 100. A quick check is to subtract 3 from the magnitude, since multiplying by 0.001 is the same as
multiplying by 10^-3 (Abramson, 2023). For your reference list, the course textbook is Abramson's
*Algebra and Trigonometry*, and adding its link would make the citation complete. How would you
explain to someone why a 7.7 is not just "a bit bigger" than a 5.7?

Best,
Nicanor
