## Peer Response 1: to Brightone Otieno

Hi Brightone,

Your examples are all correct, and I checked them: x + y = 6 and x - y = 2 do meet only at (4, 2).
One idea I found useful is that the type can be read straight from the slopes, before solving
anything (Abramson, 2021, Section 11.1). Different slopes mean one crossing point, so the system is
independent; the same slope and the same y-intercept mean the same line, so it is dependent; and
the same slope with different intercepts means parallel lines, so it is inconsistent. For your
x + y = 4 and x + y = 7, both lines have slope -1 but different intercepts, which confirms they are
parallel. It is also worth adding that a consistent system can be dependent too, not only
independent like your (3, 2) example. One small tip: your Mathispower4u references need their
YouTube links, for example https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HxhacvH49o8 for the substitution video.

## Peer Response 2: to Delissa Jonsson

Hi Delissa,

I liked that you solved every example step by step instead of just stating the answer, and all of
them check out: (4, 3) and (3, 3) satisfy their systems, and 0 = 4 clearly shows the contradiction.
Your examples also show something interesting: your consistent example is independent as well,
because consistent covers both the one-solution case and the infinitely-many case. The same
elimination idea scales up to three variables. Abramson (2021, Section 11.2) explains that if
elimination ends in an always-true statement such as 0 = 0, the system has infinitely many
solutions, and if it ends in a contradiction such as 3 = 0, it has none. I saw this in our
assignment: Daniel's toy problem turned out to be dependent, because "five times the total is
$125" just repeats "the total is $25". Have you noticed a dependent system hidden in a word
problem like that?

Best,

## Peer Response 3: to Emmanuel Odubu

Hi Emmanuel,

Your description of lines that cross, sit on top of each other, or never meet is a very clear way
to picture the three cases, and your examples x + y = 2 with x - y = 0 giving (1, 1), and
x + y = 2 with x + y = 4 having no solution, are both correct. One small fix: your dependent
example reads "2x + 2y" without its right-hand side. With "= 4" it works perfectly, since
2x + 2y = 4 is just x + y = 2 multiplied by 2. Elimination makes the difference easy to see, as
the Mathispower4u (2009) video shows: subtracting 2 times your first equation from 2x + 2y = 4
leaves 0 = 0, which is always true, while subtracting x + y = 2 from x + y = 4 leaves 0 = 2,
which is never true (Abramson, 2021, Section 11.1). Did you find graphing or elimination easier
for telling the types apart?
