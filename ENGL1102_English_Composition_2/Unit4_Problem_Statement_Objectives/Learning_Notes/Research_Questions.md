## 📚 What are Research Questions?

**Research questions** are specific inquiries that your study seeks to answer. They translate your research objectives into precise, answerable questions that guide your methodology and analysis.

**Key Relationship**:
```
Problem Statement → Objectives → Research Questions
   (WHY?)           (WHAT?)        (HOW/WHAT specifically?)
```

Research questions are the **bridge** between what you want to achieve (objectives) and how you will investigate it (methodology).

---

## 🎯 Why Are Research Questions Important?

| Function | How It Helps |
|----------|-------------|
| **Focus** | Keeps research narrowly targeted |
| **Direction** | Guides data collection and analysis |
| **Scope** | Prevents the study from becoming too broad |
| **Evaluation** | Provides criteria for assessing research success |
| **Communication** | Tells readers exactly what you're investigating |
| **Structure** | Organizes your paper around specific inquiries |

## 📝 Types of Research Questions

### 1. Descriptive Questions
**Purpose**: Describe a phenomenon, situation, or group

**Format**: "What is...?" / "What are...?" / "How does...?"

**Example**:
> What are the most common cybersecurity threats facing small businesses with fewer than 50 employees?

**Characteristics**:
- Usually answered with data, facts, or descriptions
- Good starting point for research
- Often lead to deeper analytical questions

---

### 2. Comparative Questions
**Purpose**: Compare two or more groups, concepts, or approaches

**Format**: "How does X compare to Y?" / "What are the differences between...?"

**Example**:
> How do cybersecurity breach rates compare between small businesses that invest in employee training programs versus those that rely solely on technical controls?

**Characteristics**:
- Involves at least two variables or groups
- Requires analysis of similarities and differences
- Often reveals important patterns

---

### 3. Relationship/Correlational Questions
**Purpose**: Explore connections between variables

**Format**: "What is the relationship between X and Y?" / "How does X affect Y?"

**Example**:
> What is the relationship between cybersecurity training frequency and the rate of successful phishing attacks in small businesses?

**Characteristics**:
- Examines how variables interact
- Does not necessarily imply causation
- Useful for identifying patterns and trends

---

### 4. Evaluative Questions
**Purpose**: Assess the effectiveness, value, or quality of something

**Format**: "How effective is...?" / "To what extent does...?"

**Example**:
> To what extent do existing cybersecurity frameworks (NIST, CISA) address the unique resource constraints of small businesses?

**Characteristics**:
- Requires judgment based on criteria
- Often involves assessment against standards
- Produces actionable recommendations

---

### 5. Exploratory Questions
**Purpose**: Investigate under-researched areas or generate new understanding

**Format**: "What factors contribute to...?" / "How can...?"

**Example**:
> What factors contribute to the long-term sustainability of cybersecurity training programs in resource-constrained small businesses?

**Characteristics**:
- Open-ended but focused
- Used when literature is limited
- Often leads to new frameworks or models

## 🔄 From Objectives to Questions - The Process

### Step 1: Take Each Objective

**Objective**: To analyze the current cybersecurity training approaches recommended for small businesses and their documented effectiveness.

### Step 2: Convert to Question Form

Ask: *"What do I need to find out to achieve this objective?"*

**Questions Generated**:
- What cybersecurity training approaches are currently recommended for small businesses in the literature?
- How effective have these training approaches been in reducing security incidents, based on documented evidence?

### Step 3: Ensure Alignment

Every question must:
- ✅ Connect directly to a specific objective
- ✅ Be answerable through research (not opinion)
- ✅ Be focused enough to guide investigation
- ✅ Contribute to addressing the problem statement

## ✅ Characteristics of Good Research Questions

### The FINER Framework

| Letter | Criterion | Description |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **F** | Feasible | Can be investigated with available resources |
| **I** | Interesting | Engaging to both researcher and audience |
| **N** | Novel | Contributes new knowledge or perspectives |
| **E** | Ethical | Can be answered without ethical concerns |
| **R** | Relevant | Meaningful to the field and stakeholders |

---

### Good Question Checklist

- [ ] **Clear**: No ambiguity in what's being asked
- [ ] **Focused**: Not too broad or too narrow
- [ ] **Answerable**: Can be addressed through research methods
- [ ] **Relevant**: Directly connected to an objective
- [ ] **Open-ended**: Not a simple yes/no (usually)
- [ ] **Objective**: Free from bias or leading language
- [ ] **Researchable**: Evidence exists or can be gathered

## ❌ Good vs. Bad Research Questions

### ❌ Weak Research Questions

| Question | Problem |
|----------|---------|
| "Is cybersecurity important?" | Yes/no answer, too vague |
| "Why don't businesses care about security?" | Loaded/biased assumption |
| "What is the meaning of life?" | Not researchable, too broad |
| "How can we fix everything?" | Too broad, unmeasurable |
| "Should businesses train employees?" | Opinion-based, yes/no |

### ✅ Strong Research Questions

| Question | Why It Works |
|----------|-------------|
| "What training methods most effectively reduce phishing susceptibility in small businesses?" | Specific, measurable, focused |
| "How does cybersecurity investment level correlate with breach frequency in firms with <50 employees?" | Clear variables, measurable |
| "To what extent do current frameworks address progressive implementation for resource-constrained organizations?" | Evaluative, specific scope |
| "What components are essential for a sustainable cybersecurity maturity model for small businesses?" | Exploratory, focused, novel |

## 📖 Key Concepts from Readings

### From Serdikoff - Generating Good Research Questions
- Start with **curiosity** about your topic
- Move from **broad interest** → **specific question**
- **Operationalize** your variables (define what you'll measure)
- Questions should be **empirically testable**
- Consider potential **answers** before finalizing questions
- Use **published literature** to refine your questions

### From Ryan (Scribbr) - Research Objectives
- Each objective should map to **1-2 questions**
- Questions should be **answerable within your scope**
- Use the objective's verb to frame the question
  - Objective: "To analyze..." → Question: "What does analysis reveal about...?"
  - Objective: "To compare..." → Question: "How does X compare to Y?"
  - Objective: "To assess..." → Question: "To what extent does...?"

## 💡 Pro Tips

1. **One question per inquiry**: Don't combine multiple questions into one compound question
2. **Match question type to objective**: An "analyze" objective gets analytical questions, not descriptive ones
3. **Explicitly link each question**: State "This question addresses Objective 1" in your paper
4. **Start with "What" or "How"**: These naturally produce open-ended, researchable questions
5. **Avoid "Why" for beginners**: "Why" questions are harder to answer empirically — use "What factors..." instead
6. **Test answerability**: Can you imagine what the answer might look like? If yes, it's answerable
7. **Keep parallel structure**: If your first question starts with "To what extent...", try keeping that pattern
8. **3-5 questions is ideal**: Enough to be thorough, not so many that focus is lost
