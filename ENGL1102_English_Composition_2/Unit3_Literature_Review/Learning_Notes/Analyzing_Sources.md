## 📚 What is Source Analysis?

Source analysis is the systematic evaluation of academic and professional materials to determine their:
- **Credibility**: Can you trust the information?
- **Relevance**: Does it relate to your research question?
- **Quality**: Is it well-researched and well-written?
- **Usefulness**: Will it contribute to your argument?

---

## 🎯 The CRAAP Test for Source Evaluation

### **C - Currency** (Timeliness)
**Questions to Ask:**
- When was the information published or last updated?
- Is the information current enough for your topic?
- Have newer sources superseded this information?
- Are links functional (for web sources)?

**Why It Matters:**
- In fast-moving fields (technology, medicine, cybersecurity), older sources may be outdated
- Historical topics may require older sources
- Check publication date and revision dates

**Example:**
- ✅ A 2023 article on AI ethics is current
- ❌ A 2010 article on social media trends is outdated

---

### **R - Relevance** (Importance)
**Questions to Ask:**
- Does the information relate to your topic or answer your research question?
- Who is the intended audience?
- Is the information at an appropriate level (not too elementary, not too advanced)?
- Would you be comfortable citing this source in your research paper?

**Why It Matters:**
- Not all credible sources are relevant to YOUR specific research
- Sources must directly support your thesis or argument
- Audience level affects usefulness

**Example:**
- ✅ A peer-reviewed article on "Cybersecurity Investment ROI" for a paper on business cybersecurity
- ❌ A general article on "What is Cybersecurity?" for an advanced research paper

---

### **A - Authority** (Source)
**Questions to Ask:**
- Who is the author/publisher/source/sponsor?
- What are the author's credentials or organizational affiliations?
- Is the author qualified to write on this topic?
- Is there contact information?
- Does the URL reveal anything about the source (.edu, .gov, .org, .com)?

**Why It Matters:**
- Expertise matters—a medical doctor writing about medicine vs. a blogger
- Institutional affiliation adds credibility
- Peer-reviewed sources have been vetted by experts

**Credibility Hierarchy:**
1. **Highest**: Peer-reviewed journal articles, government reports (.gov)
2. **High**: Academic books, university publications (.edu)
3. **Medium**: Professional organizations (.org), reputable news sources
4. **Low**: Personal blogs, Wikipedia, commercial sites (.com)
5. **Lowest**: Social media posts, anonymous sources

**Example:**
- ✅ Dr. Jane Smith, PhD in Cybersecurity, published in *Journal of Information Security*
- ❌ Anonymous blogger on "TechTips.com"

---

### **A - Accuracy** (Reliability)
**Questions to Ask:**
- Where does the information come from?
- Is the information supported by evidence?
- Has the information been reviewed or refereed?
- Can you verify the information in another source?
- Does the language or tone seem unbiased and free of emotion?
- Are there spelling, grammar, or typographical errors?

**Why It Matters:**
- Accurate information is verifiable and evidence-based
- Errors suggest lack of quality control
- Bias can distort information

**Red Flags:**
- No citations or references
- Sweeping generalizations without evidence
- Emotional or inflammatory language
- Numerous spelling/grammar errors
- Claims that seem too good to be true

**Example:**
- ✅ "According to a 2023 Verizon study of 16,000 breaches..." (specific, cited)
- ❌ "Everyone knows that hackers are everywhere!" (vague, emotional, no evidence)

---

### **P - Purpose** (Reason)
**Questions to Ask:**
- What is the purpose of the information? (inform, teach, sell, entertain, persuade)
- Do the authors/sponsors make their intentions clear?
- Is the information fact, opinion, or propaganda?
- Does the point of view appear objective and impartial?
- Are there political, ideological, cultural, religious, institutional, or personal biases?

**Why It Matters:**
- Purpose affects objectivity
- Commercial sources may prioritize sales over accuracy
- Advocacy sources may present one-sided arguments

**Purpose Types:**
- **Inform/Educate**: Academic journals, textbooks, government reports
- **Persuade/Advocate**: Opinion pieces, editorials, advocacy group publications
- **Sell/Market**: Company websites, advertisements, sponsored content
- **Entertain**: Popular media, blogs, social media

**Example:**
- ✅ NIST Cybersecurity Framework (purpose: inform and guide)
- ❌ "Buy Our Security Software Now!" (purpose: sell)

## 🔍 Critical Reading Strategies

### 1. **Preview the Source**
- Read title, abstract, introduction, conclusion
- Scan headings and subheadings
- Look at figures, tables, and charts
- Check references/bibliography

### 2. **Identify the Main Argument**
- What is the author's thesis or main claim?
- What evidence supports this claim?
- What are the key findings or conclusions?

### 3. **Evaluate the Evidence**
- Is the evidence sufficient and appropriate?
- Are research methods sound?
- Is the sample size adequate?
- Are statistics used correctly?

### 4. **Consider Context**
- When was this written? (historical context)
- Who funded the research? (potential bias)
- What was the research setting? (generalizability)

### 5. **Question and Challenge**
- What assumptions does the author make?
- Are there alternative explanations?
- What are the limitations?
- What questions remain unanswered?

## 📝 Taking Analytical Notes

### Annotation Strategy
When reading sources, note:
- **Main Ideas**: What is the central argument?
- **Key Evidence**: What data/examples support the argument?
- **Methodology**: How was the research conducted?
- **Strengths**: What does this source do well?
- **Limitations**: What are the weaknesses or gaps?
- **Connections**: How does this relate to other sources?
- **Quotes**: Mark passages you might quote (with page numbers)
- **Questions**: What remains unclear or unexplored?

### Sample Annotation
**Source**: Eling & Schnell (2016) - Cyber Risk Management

**Main Idea**: Cyber risk management requires quantitative assessment methods

**Key Evidence**: Analysis of 1,000+ cyber incidents shows financial impact patterns

**Methodology**: Quantitative analysis of insurance claims data

**Strengths**: Large dataset, rigorous statistical analysis, practical implications

**Limitations**: Focus on insured losses only, may not capture all cyber risks

**Connections**: Complements NIST framework (qualitative) with quantitative approach

**Quote**: "Organizations that quantify cyber risk show 40% better incident response" (p. 15)

**Questions**: How do small businesses without insurance data assess risk?

## 🎓 Academic vs. Non-Academic Sources

### Academic Sources (Scholarly)
**Characteristics:**
- Written by experts (PhDs, researchers)
- Peer-reviewed before publication
- Include citations and references
- Published in academic journals or university presses
- Use formal, technical language
- Focus on original research or theory

**Examples:**
- Journal articles (*Journal of Cybersecurity*)
- Academic books (university press)
- Conference proceedings (IEEE, ACM)
- Dissertations and theses

**When to Use:**
- Research papers requiring scholarly evidence
- Literature reviews
- Theoretical discussions
- Establishing credibility

---

### Professional/Trade Sources
**Characteristics:**
- Written by industry professionals
- May be peer-reviewed or editorially reviewed
- Include some citations
- Published in trade journals or professional magazines
- Use industry-specific language
- Focus on practical applications

**Examples:**
- *Harvard Business Review*
- Industry reports (Verizon DBIR)
- Professional association publications
- White papers from reputable companies

**When to Use:**
- Practical applications
- Industry trends
- Real-world examples
- Current practices

---

### Popular Sources
**Characteristics:**
- Written by journalists or staff writers
- Editorially reviewed (not peer-reviewed)
- Few or no citations
- Published in newspapers, magazines, websites
- Use accessible language
- Focus on current events or general interest

**Examples:**
- *The New York Times*
- *Wired Magazine*
- News websites
- General interest blogs

**When to Use:**
- Background information
- Current events
- General audience perspective
- Supplementary examples (not primary evidence)

## ⚠️ Red Flags: Sources to Avoid

1. **No Author Listed** (anonymous sources)
2. **No Publication Date** (can't assess currency)
3. **No Citations/References** (can't verify claims)
4. **Obvious Bias** (one-sided, emotional language)
5. **Commercial Agenda** (primary purpose is to sell)
6. **Poor Quality** (spelling errors, broken links, unprofessional design)
7. **Unverifiable Claims** (can't find supporting evidence elsewhere)
8. **Wikipedia** (as a primary source—use it for background, then find original sources)
9. **Social Media Posts** (unless analyzing social media itself)
10. **Predatory Journals** (pay-to-publish with no peer review)

## ✅ Source Analysis Checklist

Before using a source in your research, ask:

- [ ] Is the author qualified to write on this topic?
- [ ] Is the publication venue reputable?
- [ ] Is the information current enough for my topic?
- [ ] Does the source directly relate to my research question?
- [ ] Is the information supported by evidence?
- [ ] Can I verify the information in other sources?
- [ ] Is the purpose clear and appropriate?
- [ ] Is the tone objective and professional?
- [ ] Are there citations and references?
- [ ] Would my instructor approve this source?
