Giovanni, your abstract handles a genuinely complex topic well — the tension between social media as a harm and as a support resource is difficult to hold in balance within a short summary, and you manage it without oversimplifying either side. The findings section is particularly strong: rather than hedging, you commit to a clear conclusion that excessive use is "consistently associated with increased stress, anxiety, and lower self-confidence," which is exactly the kind of decisive language Serdikoff (2021) recommends for the findings element — stating not just what was found but what it implies for practice (pp. 121–122).

One area to consider tightening is the problem statement. Currently it spans two paragraphs and blends background context with the specific gap the study addresses. A sharper single-paragraph problem statement — one that explicitly names what the existing literature has *not yet resolved* — would make the abstract easier to scan. For instance, you could specify that while correlational studies link heavy usage to anxiety, the literature lacks consensus on whether the relationship is causal or moderated by usage type (passive scrolling versus active engagement). That distinction would give your research question more precise grounding.

Your three research questions are well-chosen, but the third — whether social media can function as an emotional support tool — reads slightly broader than the first two. Narrowing it to a specific population or usage pattern (e.g., adolescents using peer support communities versus general browsing) would align it more tightly with your qualitative methodology.

Overall, this is a well-written abstract that covers all six required elements and demonstrates genuine engagement with the psychological literature.
