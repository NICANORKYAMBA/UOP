# Unit 7 Learning Notes — Writing an Effective Abstract

**Course**: ENGL1102 English Composition 2  
**Unit**: 7 — Composing Abstracts  
**Sources**: Serdikoff (2021); Writing Center, UW–Madison (n.d.)

---

## 1. What Is an Abstract?

An abstract is a concise, self-contained summary of a research paper. It appears at the beginning of the paper but is written last, after the full paper is complete. Its purpose is to allow readers to quickly determine whether the paper is relevant to their needs without reading the entire document.

The Writing Center at the University of Wisconsin–Madison (n.d.) defines an abstract as "a brief, comprehensive summary of the contents of the article" that enables readers to survey the literature efficiently.

---

## 2. What to Include in an Abstract

A well-written research abstract typically covers six elements:

| Element | What It Answers |
|---|---|
| **Background/Context** | Why does this topic matter? What is the broader problem? |
| **Problem Statement** | What specific gap or issue does this research address? |
| **Research Objectives** | What does the study aim to achieve? |
| **Research Questions** | What specific questions guide the investigation? |
| **Methodology** | How was the research conducted? What approach/design? |
| **Main Findings/Conclusions** | What did the research find or propose? (if applicable) |

---

## 3. What to Omit from an Abstract

- Detailed literature citations (no in-text citations in the abstract itself)
- Tables, figures, or lengthy examples
- Background information not directly relevant to the study
- Jargon or undefined abbreviations
- Anything not covered in the paper itself

---

## 4. Types of Abstracts

| Type | Description | When Used |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | Describes what the paper covers without giving results | Short papers, humanities |
| **Informative** | Includes background, methods, results, and conclusions | Most research papers |
| **Structured** | Uses explicit headings (Background, Methods, Results) | Medical/scientific journals |

For academic research papers, the **informative abstract** is standard.

---

## 5. Key Principles from Serdikoff (2021)

Serdikoff (2021) provides an example abstract on pages 121–122 that demonstrates:
- Opening with the research problem and its significance
- Stating the methodology concisely (one to two sentences)
- Summarizing key findings without excessive detail
- Keeping the entire abstract to 150–250 words for most papers

---

## 6. Writing Process

1. **Write the paper first** — the abstract summarizes what already exists
2. **Identify one sentence** for each of the six elements above
3. **Draft in order**: background → problem → objectives → questions → method → findings
4. **Cut ruthlessly** — every word must earn its place
5. **Read it standalone** — it must make sense without the paper

---

## 7. Language Tips

- Use present tense for general facts and conclusions
- Use past tense for what the study did ("this study examined...")
- Use active voice where possible ("this study proposes..." not "a proposal is made...")
- Avoid vague openers like "This paper discusses..." — be specific immediately

---

## 8. Word Count Guidelines

| Context | Typical Length |
|---|---|
| Journal article | 150–250 words |
| Dissertation/thesis | 300–500 words |
| Conference paper | 100–150 words |
| UoPeople ENGL1102 Unit 7 | 250–500 words (per assignment) |
