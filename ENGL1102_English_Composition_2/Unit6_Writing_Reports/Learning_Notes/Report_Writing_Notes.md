## 1. The Scientific Process: Analyzing Data (Section 16)

After collecting data, researchers must analyze it systematically to extract meaning. For quantitative studies, this involves:

- **Descriptive statistics**: summarizing data with percentages, means, frequencies
- **Visual representation**: charts, graphs, tables that make patterns visible at a glance
- **Pattern identification**: noting which categories dominate, which are minimal, and what relationships exist between variables

Serdikoff (2021) notes that data analysis transforms raw numbers into interpretable findings — the bridge between what was measured and what it means (Section 16).

---

## 2. Drawing Conclusions and Reporting Results (Section 17)

Reporting results requires more than listing numbers. Effective reporting:

- **Interprets** what the data shows, not just what it says
- **Connects** findings to the research question or real-world context
- **Acknowledges limitations** — what the data cannot tell us
- **Avoids overgeneralizing** — staying within the scope of the data

Serdikoff (2021) emphasizes that conclusions must be grounded in the data and not extend beyond what the evidence supports (Section 17).

## 3. Structure of a Formal Report

Based on College of Business and Economics (2024) guidelines:

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Identifies the topic and scope |
| **Introduction** | States the purpose, context, and data source |
| **Findings** | Presents and interprets the data — the core section |
| **Discussion/Analysis** | Explains patterns, comparisons, implications |
| **Conclusion** | Summarizes key takeaways and significance |
| **References** | APA-formatted sources |

## 4. Analyzing a Pie Chart

When interpreting a pie chart in a report:

1. **Identify the largest segment** — what activity/category dominates?
2. **Identify the smallest segment** — what is least represented?
3. **Group related segments** — e.g., all work-related vs. leisure activities
4. **Compare to expectations** — does the data confirm or challenge assumptions?
5. **Consider the population** — who was surveyed? What are the implications?
6. **Note what is missing** — what categories are not represented?

### Language for Describing Chart Data

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Largest share | "accounts for the largest proportion," "dominates at X%" |
| Smallest share | "represents the smallest segment," "constitutes only X%" |
| Comparison | "compared to," "in contrast," "while X accounts for…, Y accounts for…" |
| Trend/implication | "this suggests," "these findings indicate," "notably" |
| Limitation | "the data does not account for," "it is important to note that" |

## 5. BLS American Time Use Survey — Key Data Points

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) American Time Use Survey measures how Americans spend their time on an average day. Key categories from the pie chart:

- **Sleeping**: largest single activity (~8.8 hours / ~37%)
- **Leisure and sports**: second largest (~5.0 hours / ~21%)
- **Working and work-related**: (~3.6 hours / ~15% — averaged across all days including non-workdays)
- **Household activities**: (~1.8 hours / ~8%)
- **Eating and drinking**: (~1.1 hours / ~5%)
- **Caring for others**: (~1.1 hours / ~5%)
- **Other**: remaining categories (education, purchasing goods, etc.)

> Note: BLS figures are averaged across all Americans aged 15+, including weekends and non-employed individuals, which explains why "working" appears lower than expected.

## 6. Writing Tips for This Assignment

- Open with the data source and purpose — do not assume the reader has seen the chart
- Use specific percentages when describing segments — vague language weakens analysis
- Move from description → interpretation → implication in each paragraph
- Connect findings to broader social or economic context (e.g., work-life balance, public health)
- Stay within 350–750 words — be precise, not exhaustive
- Every claim about the data needs a citation to the BLS source
