## Peer Response

Hi Thong,

Your discussion on children's mental resilience raises a compelling question that many educators, parents, and psychologists are grappling with today. I appreciate how you drew from personal high school experiences to ground your brainstorming process—this connection between observation and research is exactly what the scientific method encourages (University of Nevada Cooperative Extension, n.d.). Your hypothesis linking smartphone use and reduced outdoor play to higher anxiety is both testable and timely, especially given the growing body of research on digital technology's impact on youth mental health.

Your observation about how children in the past shared similar stressful experiences—like strict punishment—and normalized them through peer interaction is particularly insightful. The example of children comparing "battle scars" from discipline creates an interesting contrast with today's isolated digital experiences. This communal processing of stress versus individual internalization is a crucial distinction that strengthens your argument. As you noted, when stress is shared and validated by peers in real-life settings, it becomes more manageable (Haidt, 2023).

However, I'd like to offer a perspective that might add complexity to your analysis. While you correctly identify that modern children face different stressors—academic pressure, social media comparison, reduced break time—it's worth considering whether children are actually "less resilient" or simply facing *qualitatively different* challenges that require different coping mechanisms. The resilience needed to endure physical punishment differs fundamentally from the resilience needed to navigate cyberbullying, constant social comparison, and the pressure of curated online identities.

**Here's a question to deepen your research**: Could it be that we're measuring resilience using outdated metrics? Traditional resilience often emphasized stoicism and physical toughness, but modern challenges require emotional intelligence, digital literacy, and the ability to manage information overload. Research by Rutter (2012) suggests that resilience is context-dependent—what builds resilience in one environment may not transfer to another. Perhaps today's children aren't weaker; they're simply unprepared for challenges their parents never faced.

Your methodology of comparing children with high outdoor play versus high social media use is solid, but consider adding a third variable: *quality of social connections*. Your discussion mentions that teens "keep problems to themselves," but some research indicates that online communities can provide support networks for marginalized youth who lack in-person connections (boyd, 2014). The issue might not be technology itself, but rather how it's used and whether it supplements or replaces face-to-face interaction.

Additionally, your point about parents "stealing break time" with extra classes resonates strongly, especially in cultures with high academic expectations. This raises an important question: Is the problem technology, or is it the broader cultural shift toward achievement-focused parenting that leaves no room for unstructured play and emotional recovery? Twenge (2017) found that increased academic pressure correlates with rising anxiety rates, independent of smartphone use.

Your brainstorming process effectively identified multiple contributing factors—smartphones, parenting styles, academic pressure, reduced physical play, and social isolation. As you develop this research further, consider exploring how these factors interact rather than treating them as separate causes. For instance, does academic pressure drive excessive phone use as an escape mechanism? Do overprotective parenting styles prevent children from developing coping skills they'd naturally acquire through outdoor play and peer conflict?

Thank you for tackling such a relevant and complex topic. Your discussion has important implications for how we structure childhood experiences, design educational systems, and integrate technology into young people's lives. I'm curious to see how your research evolves!
