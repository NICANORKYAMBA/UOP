## Response to Happy Madziba

Hi Happy,

Your email is professionally drafted and your justification for choosing a mixed-methods approach is compelling. The way you connected the SBA's 20% first-year failure statistic to the need for qualitative accounts of resilience is particularly effective — it demonstrates exactly why neither approach alone would be sufficient for your research goals.

I want to build on your point about balancing qualitative and quantitative data, which you identified as a key concern. One practical strategy is to use a **sequential explanatory design**, where the quantitative phase is conducted first to identify patterns — such as which industries or funding levels correlate with higher failure rates — and the qualitative phase follows to explain those patterns through entrepreneur interviews. This sequencing ensures that your interview questions are informed by the statistical findings, making the qualitative data more targeted and analytically powerful. Serdikoff (2021) notes that this kind of integration strengthens the overall rigor of mixed-methods research by ensuring each phase builds meaningfully on the other (Section 36).

Regarding your concern about rigor, one important consideration is **purposive sampling** for the qualitative component. Rather than interviewing entrepreneurs randomly, selecting participants who represent the range of outcomes identified in your quantitative data — both those who failed and those who survived — would ensure that your qualitative findings are grounded in the statistical reality of your sample (National University, 2024). This approach directly addresses the tension between depth and generalizability that mixed-methods research must navigate.

Your email is clear, concise, and well-organized — a strong model of professional academic communication.
