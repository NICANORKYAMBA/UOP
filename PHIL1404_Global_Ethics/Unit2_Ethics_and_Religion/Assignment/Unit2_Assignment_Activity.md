Can people of different faiths, or of no faith, agree on what is right? Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) answered yes. His natural law theory holds that God built moral order into human nature, so anyone who reasons carefully can discover it (Morgan, 2019). This paper explains the principles of Aquinas's natural law, presents three objections with replies, explores why a follower of the theory should be moral, and reflects on how it fits my own beliefs.

## The Principles of Aquinas's Natural Law

**Law as participation in God's reason.** Aquinas describes God's plan for the whole universe as the eternal law. Every creature follows that plan, but humans share in it in a special way, through reason. This rational share in the eternal law is the natural law (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 91, a. 2). Natural law therefore does not compete with religion; it is the part of God's plan that human reason can read for itself.

**The first precept.** Just as all theoretical thinking rests on the principle that a thing cannot both be and not be, all practical thinking rests on one self-evident rule: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 2). Every other moral rule is a way of applying this one.

**The primary precepts.** Reason recognizes as good whatever humans are naturally inclined toward, and Aquinas orders these inclinations in three levels (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 2):

1. Like all living things, we seek to preserve our lives, so protecting life is good and murder is wrong.
2. Like other animals, we reproduce and raise offspring, so caring for and educating children is good.
3. As rational beings, we seek truth, including truth about God, and a shared life with others, so learning, honesty, and not harming our neighbours are good.

**Secondary precepts and their limits.** More specific rules, such as "return what you borrow," follow from the primary precepts. Aquinas notes that these hold in most cases but can fail in unusual ones; for example, returning a borrowed weapon to someone who wants to use it against his country would be wrong (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 4). The general principles never change, but applying them takes judgment.

**Purpose in nature.** Following Aristotle, Aquinas holds that everything has a final cause, a purpose that reason can discover. Teeth are for chewing and the heart is for pumping blood, and the right use of a thing follows its purpose (Morgan, 2019).

**The doctrine of double effect.** Some acts have two effects, one good and one bad. Aquinas argued that a person may defend themselves even if the attacker dies, provided the death is not what they intend but a side effect of protecting their life (Morgan, 2019). Today hospitals use the same idea when a doctor gives strong pain relief to a dying patient, intending to ease pain even though the medicine may shorten life.

## Objections and Replies

**Objection 1: Facts cannot produce values.** David Hume argued that we cannot move from what "is" to what "ought" to be, and G. E. Moore called such reasoning the naturalistic fallacy. Natural law seems to observe a biological fact, for example that sex leads to reproduction, and then conclude how sex ought to be used (Morgan, 2019).

*Reply.* Defenders answer that Aquinas does not start from bare biology. His first precept is already practical: it is a self-evident truth about the good, grasped by reason, not a conclusion drawn from scientific facts (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 2). Contemporary natural law theorists likewise treat basic goods such as life, knowledge, and friendship as known directly by practical reason, which avoids Hume's charge (Murphy, 2025).

**Objection 2: Things have more than one purpose.** Modern science explains nature without purposes, and even if something has a function, it does not follow that this is its only proper use. Sex can strengthen love between partners as well as produce children (Morgan, 2019).

*Reply.* This is the strongest objection, and it hits some of Aquinas's specific conclusions, especially in sexual ethics. However, natural law theorists can accept that a thing has several goods while still holding that some uses are wrong, such as using another person purely as an object. The core of the theory, that human flourishing has a shape we can know, survives even if some of Aquinas's applications do not.

**Objection 3: Cultures disagree about morality.** If the natural law is written in everyone's reason, why do societies hold such different moral codes?

*Reply.* Aquinas anticipated this. He argued that the general principles are known by all, but detailed conclusions can be distorted by passion, bad habits, or corrupt customs; his own example is that theft was once accepted among the ancient Germans, although it is contrary to natural law (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 4). Disagreement shows that moral knowledge can be clouded, not that there is no moral truth, just as students can get a maths problem wrong without the problem lacking an answer.

## Why Should a Follower Be Moral?

For Aquinas, being moral is not obeying an outside authority for its own sake; it is becoming what we are made to be. Because good is what all things seek, acting well fulfills our nature, and acting badly works against it (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 2). Aquinas joins Aristotle's idea that good acts are those that lead to human flourishing with Christian theology (Floyd, n.d.). A person who lies or harms others damages the social and rational nature that makes their own life go well.

This rationale is deeply religious, but not arbitrary. The order in nature comes from a wise and good God, so following it means sharing in God's own reason (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 91, a. 2). This is how Aquinas avoids the Euthyphro dilemma: God commands acts because they are good, and they are good because they fit the nature God created, so God's commands are neither arbitrary nor unnecessary (Dimmock & Fisher, 2017; Morgan, 2019). God's commands also act as a "failsafe," since human reasoning can go wrong while God's cannot (Morgan, 2019). Finally, Aquinas holds that complete happiness cannot be reached in this life; it lies in beatitude, a supernatural union with God, who alone is perfectly good (Floyd, n.d.). Living morally is the path toward that end.

## Personal Reflection

Much of Aquinas's theory resonates with me. In Kenya, people from many ethnic communities and faiths share core values such as protecting children, caring for elders, and welcoming guests. Natural law explains this agreement well: these values come from a common human nature, not from one group's customs. I also find it compelling that the theory lets believers and non-believers reason together, which matters in a diverse country. In my work as a DevOps engineer, I see a similar logic in good system design: each component has a purpose, and misusing it causes failures elsewhere.

Yet the same analogy shows where I disagree. Engineers often find valuable new uses for tools beyond their original purpose, and I think human life is similar. I am not convinced that each part of us has only one proper use, so I find Aquinas's sexual ethics hard to accept. The doctrine of double effect also troubles me, because it depends on a person's private intention, which is difficult to verify; two doctors can perform the same act with different intentions, and only one is considered right.

Overall, I accept natural law's central claims, that morality is objective, rooted in human flourishing, and knowable by reason, while rejecting some of Aquinas's specific conclusions. Like the theory itself, I think moral understanding grows as reason corrects old applications without abandoning its first principle: do good and avoid evil.
