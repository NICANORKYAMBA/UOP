## Peer Response 1: to Ahmed Ali

Hi Ahmed,

Your point that religious and secular people can share moral ground fits well with Aquinas. In the Summa, he argues that the most general principles of the natural law are the same for everyone, while more detailed conclusions can be clouded by passion, habit, or custom (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 4). That helps explain your same-sex marriage example: both sides agree on basic goods such as dignity, justice, and the freedom to live by conscience, but they disagree sharply on how those goods apply to marriage. The American solution you describe, protecting civil rights while allowing some religious exemptions, seems to accept that shared principles do not guarantee shared conclusions. Do you think exemptions like these can last, or will one side eventually have to give way?

References

Aquinas, T. (1920). *Summa theologica* (Fathers of the English Dominican Province, Trans.; 2nd rev. ed.). New Advent. https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2094.htm (Original work written 1265-1274)

## Peer Response 2: to Obisanya Tunde

Hi Obisanya,

I found your example about respecting elders without giving up your conscience very honest, and I agree that respect should not mean blind obedience. Aquinas actually makes a similar point: he says the general principles of the natural law are known to everyone, but bad customs can distort how people apply them, and he gives the example of theft once being accepted among the ancient Germans even though it is against natural law (Aquinas, 1920, I-II, q. 94, a. 4). By that reasoning, keeping girls out of school is not a true expression of faith but a custom that has drifted away from the good of human flourishing. How do you think young people can raise these concerns in Nigeria without being seen as disrespectful to their elders?

## Peer Response 3: to James Ogunde

Hi James,

Your abortion example is a strong choice, and I like that you quoted Article 26(4) directly, because it shows how Kenya tries to hold both convictions in one legal text. Interestingly, the natural law tradition has its own tool for emergency cases: the doctrine of double effect. Morgan (2019) explains that an act with a good and a bad effect can be permitted when the bad effect is foreseen but not intended, as when a doctor relieves a patient's pain knowing it may shorten life. Some religious thinkers use this reasoning to accept life-saving treatment for a mother even when the pregnancy is lost, which shows that religious and medical views may be closer in emergencies than the public debate suggests. Do you think double effect could help Kenyan religious leaders and health workers find more common ground?

References

Morgan, J. (2019). Can we have ethics without religion? On divine command theory and natural law theory. In G. Matthews & C. Hendricks (Eds.), *Introduction to philosophy: Ethics*.
