## My Metaethical View

I believe morality is objective, but its rightness does not come from religious commands. The Euthyphro dilemma shapes my view: if an act is wrong only because God forbids it, God's commands look arbitrary, but if God forbids it because it is wrong, then there is a moral standard we can understand on its own (Morgan, 2019). I take the second option. Cruelty is wrong because of what it does to a person, and a good God would forbid it for that reason.

This does not make religion useless to morality. Benitez (2020) argues that God's commands are not arbitrary because they flow from God's perfectly good character, much as a kind friend acts kindly because of who they are. I find this a fair reply, and it still fits my view, because it treats goodness as something real that commands express rather than create. The religious view I find most convincing is Robert Adams' modified divine command theory, which starts from confidence in basic moral facts, such as the wrongness of needless suffering, and treats a loving God as their best explanation rather than their arbitrary source (Morgan, 2019). I am closest to natural law thinking: moral truths are built into human nature and can be known by reason, so believers and non-believers can reach them together (Morgan, 2019). Religion, for me, is a valuable source of moral wisdom and motivation, but not the only door to moral knowledge.

## A Kenyan Conflict: Faith, Freedom, and Shakahola

In 2023, police in Kenya's Shakahola forest began finding the bodies of followers of preacher Paul Mackenzie, who had been urged to starve themselves and their children as a path to salvation. More than 400 bodies were exhumed and hundreds of people were reported missing (Houghton, 2024). Here a religious belief, that fasting to death leads to salvation, collided directly with a moral conviction almost every Kenyan shares, that innocent life, especially a child's life, must be protected.

The dynamics are difficult because Kenya is a secular state with many faiths. Article 32 of the Constitution protects everyone's right to practise a religion of their choice (Houghton, 2024). Most Kenyans are Christian, with Muslim, Hindu, and traditional believers alongside them, so any rule that lets the state judge "true" religion threatens minority faiths. On the other hand, unregulated freedom let one preacher lead hundreds to their deaths.

Kenya addressed this through both politics and law. A Senate committee investigated security failures, and a presidential taskforce chaired by Rev. Mutava Musyimi collected views across the country. It recommended a Religious Organisations Bill and a Registrar of Religious Organisations who could deregister groups linked to extremism, while protecting freedom of religion ("Shakahola probe team," 2024). Its model is hybrid: faith communities regulate themselves through umbrella bodies, with government oversight. Critics such as Amnesty International Kenya warned that the plan offers few new ideas and closely copies the 1990 law that regulates NGOs (Houghton, 2024). The multicultural lesson is that the state can protect life without choosing between religions, by judging harmful actions rather than beliefs.

## Does Religion Help Morality Thrive?

Robert Adams argues that without "a moral order of the universe," people can become "demoralized": if the world is indifferent, why keep making sacrifices that may come to nothing? Theistic religion promises that moral effort contributes to a good world-history (Morgan, 2019).

I think Adams is partly right. Religion gives morality a rich context in which it can thrive: communities that care for the poor, stories of moral heroes, and teachings that push people beyond the standards of their time, such as Christ's command to love one's neighbour or the Qur'an's call to appreciate diversity (Morgan, 2019). In Kenya, churches and mosques run many schools, hospitals, and relief efforts.

However, Shakahola shows the danger in Adams' argument. The same hope in a final reward that keeps people moral can be exploited, so faith helps morality thrive only when it stays answerable to reason and to the shared conviction that life matters. And millions of non-religious people live moral lives without that assurance (Morgan, 2019). Religion can help morality flourish, but it is not required for it.

**Question for the class:** How can a country protect freedom of religion while stopping harmful practices, without deciding which beliefs are "true"?
