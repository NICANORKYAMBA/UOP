## Peer Response 1: to Keerthana Chandrakumar

Hi Keerthana,

Your elder care example is a great case of what Rezkalla (2019) means when he says many moral differences are not really disagreements about values. Sri Lankan families and Western families who choose residential care both value caring for their parents; they disagree mostly about how that care is best given, which fits his senicide example, where a shocking practice was actually rooted in care for the elderly. I also liked that you pointed out daughters and daughters-in-law carry most of the work, because it shows a good value can still be shared unfairly inside a culture, which is close to Sikka's (2012) point about power. Do you think the state should support family caregivers so the duty does not fall mainly on women?

References

Rezkalla, P. (2019). Aren't right and wrong just matters of opinion? On moral relativism and subjectivism. In G. Matthews & C. Hendricks (Eds.), *Introduction to philosophy: Ethics*. ## Peer Response 2: to Abubakar Maryam

Hi Abubakar,

I wrote about bride price in Kenya too, so it was interesting to see how close the Nigerian meaning is: respect, appreciation, and joining two families rather than buying a wife. Your point that problems start when the practice becomes too expensive matches what I found. A Kenyan report by KELIN (n.d.) shows that whether dowry was completed can decide if a widow keeps her matrimonial land, and courts have treated unreturned dowry as proof that a marriage still exists. That made me agree with your view that the practice is meaningful only while it protects the woman's dignity and choice. In Nigeria, is bride price usually refunded after a divorce, and do you think that makes it harder for women to leave an unhappy marriage?

## Peer Response 3: to Chinedu Ashinze

Hi Chinedu,

Your greeting example is a clear case of what the McCombs School of Business (2019) calls pluralism: different ways of showing respect existing side by side, which does not mean that morality itself is relative. Kneeling before an elder in Nigeria and using a first name in some Western countries both aim at respect; only the custom differs. To answer your question, I think the line you drew is the right one, between harmless differences like greetings and practices that seriously harm people who did not consent. I would add that change is most legitimate when it comes from people inside the culture, with outsiders supporting them rather than giving orders. Who do you think should lead that kind of reform in a community?

References

McCombs School of Business. (2019, February 19). *All is not relative | Concepts unwrapped* [Video]. YouTube. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z11v2nWsgGA
