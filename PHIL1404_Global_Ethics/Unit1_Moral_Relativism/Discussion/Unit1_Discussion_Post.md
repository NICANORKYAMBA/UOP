## How I See Ethics: Realism or Relativism?

I see ethics as the study of how we ought to treat each other and why, which matches the way the McCombs School of Business (2018) describes moral philosophy: metaethics asks what morality is, normative ethics gives us frameworks for deciding, and applied ethics deals with real cases. For me, ethics is not only a list of rules my community hands down. It is a set of reasons that anyone, from any background, should be able to examine and test.

Based on this, I align more with moral realism. Rezkalla (2019) defines moral realism as the view that some moral facts hold and bind us even when people believe otherwise, while relativism makes moral truth depend on the culture that holds a belief. I hold a moderate version of realism. I believe a small core of moral claims is true everywhere, such as the claim that torturing children for fun or enslaving people is wrong, while many practices around marriage, food, dress, and greeting can rightly vary. Three points from the reading convinced me. First, disagreement does not prove there is no right answer, just as students getting different answers to a quadratic equation does not mean the equation has no solution (Rezkalla, 2019). Second, many apparent moral disagreements are really disagreements about facts, as in the senicide example, where the practice rests on beliefs about the afterlife rather than a lack of care for the elderly. Third, if relativism were true, the end of slavery would only be moral change, not moral progress, and I cannot accept that. The McCombs School of Business (2019) also helped me see that accepting many viewpoints in one society is pluralism, which is not the same as saying all views are equally correct.

## A Kenyan Example: Bride Price

In Kenya, many communities practice bride price, often called dowry locally (ruracio among the Kikuyu, for example). The groom's family gives livestock, money, or other gifts to the bride's family through a series of visits and negotiations. Many Kenyans see this as deeply ethical. It shows respect and gratitude to the family that raised the bride, proves the groom's seriousness and ability to provide, and joins two families, not just two people (Kenya Legal and Ethical Issues Network on HIV and AIDS [KELIN], n.d.). The law reflects this: under the Marriage Act of 2014, where a community's customs require dowry, payment of a token is enough to prove a customary marriage (KELIN, n.d.).

In many Western countries, people who hear about bride price see something unethical: a payment that looks like buying a wife and treating a woman as property. The cultural context explains the gap. In Kenya, marriage has traditionally been an agreement between families and clans in mostly rural, livestock-based economies, so the gifts express relationships and obligations. In more individualistic societies, marriage is a private contract between two people, and money changing hands between families looks like a sale.

The implications are serious on both sides. Bride price builds strong family networks that support couples, but it can also harm women. KELIN (n.d.) reports that whether dowry was completed can decide if a widow keeps her matrimonial land, and courts have used unreturned dowry to settle disputes over a woman's marriage and property. High prices can also delay marriage for young men. This shows that a practice can carry good values (respect, family unity) and still produce outcomes that need reform from within.

## Does One Culture Have a Right to Judge Another?

I disagree with normative relativism. Its central rule, that no culture should judge or interfere with another, is itself a universal moral claim, which is exactly what relativists say does not exist (Rezkalla, 2019). Also, tolerance cannot be the highest value without limits, because some practices, like child abuse, slavery, and oppression of the vulnerable, do not deserve tolerance.

The idea of "a culture" is also unclear. Rezkalla (2019) asks how many people a culture needs, using the American abolitionists, who opposed the majority view of their own country. Sikka (2012) adds that cultural norms are often shaped by those in power, so "respecting a culture" can mean respecting its elites while ignoring the women, minorities, or poor members who disagree. Refusing to judge can therefore mean taking the side of the powerful.

Still, I take the main counterargument seriously. Outsiders have often judged other cultures out of ignorance or self-interest. The British banned sati in 1829 on moral grounds (Rezkalla, 2019), yet colonial powers also used claims of moral superiority to justify ruling other peoples, including in Kenya. Many outsiders who condemn bride price also misunderstand what it means to the families involved.

So I believe one culture may judge another, but only under clear conditions:

1. The practice causes serious harm to people who did not consent or cannot leave, such as children, widows, or minorities.
2. The judgment rests on reasons the culture's own members could accept, such as human dignity and well-being, not on "our way is better."
3. The judge first gets the facts right, since many apparent moral differences are really factual ones.
4. Judging means dialogue and support for reformers inside the culture, not domination or forced change.

By this standard, cultures may judge sati or slavery, while bride price itself should be respected, and its harmful uses, like taking land from widows, should be challenged mainly by Kenyans themselves.
