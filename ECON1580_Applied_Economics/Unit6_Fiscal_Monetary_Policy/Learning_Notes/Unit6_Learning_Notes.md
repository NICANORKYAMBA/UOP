## Chapter 22: Inflation

### 22.1 — Tracking Inflation

**What is Inflation?**
- Inflation = sustained rise in the overall price level of goods and services
- Measured using **index numbers** — compare the cost of a "basket of goods" over time
- **Base year**: the reference year assigned an index value of 100
- Formula: Inflation Rate = ((Index Year 2 - Index Year 1) / Index Year 1) × 100

**Index Numbers**
- Simplify complex price data into a single number
- Allow comparison across time periods
- Example: if basket costs $100 in base year and $105 next year → index = 105, inflation = 5%

**Limitations of Index Numbers**
- Fixed basket may not reflect actual consumer behavior
- Quality changes not always captured
- New products not immediately included

---

### 22.2 — How to Measure Changes in the Cost of Living

**Consumer Price Index (CPI)**
- Most widely used measure of inflation
- Tracks price changes for a fixed basket of goods/services purchased by typical urban consumers
- Calculated monthly by Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)

**CPI Biases and Adjustments**
- **Substitution bias**: consumers switch to cheaper alternatives when prices rise — CPI overstates cost increase
- **Quality/new goods bias**: improvements in product quality mean higher prices don't always mean higher cost of living
- **Outlet substitution bias**: consumers shift to discount stores

**Other Price Indices**
| Index | What It Measures |
|-------|-----------------|
| CPI | Consumer goods and services |
| Producer Price Index (PPI) | Wholesale/producer prices |
| Employment Cost Index (ECI) | Labor costs (wages + benefits) |
| GDP Deflator | All goods/services in GDP |

---

### 22.3 — How the U.S. and Other Countries Experience Inflation

- U.S. inflation has been moderate (2-4%) since the 1990s
- Historical extremes: double-digit inflation in 1970s-80s, deflation during Great Depression
- International variation: hyperinflation in Zimbabwe, Venezuela; deflation in Japan
- Developing countries tend to experience higher and more volatile inflation

---

### 22.4 — The Confusion Over Inflation

**Redistribution Effects**
- Inflation redistributes purchasing power from creditors to debtors (debt is repaid in cheaper dollars)
- Fixed-income earners (retirees, bondholders) lose purchasing power
- Wage earners may lag behind if wages don't adjust quickly

**Market Signal Distortion**
- Inflation makes it harder to distinguish between relative price changes and general price level changes
- Complicates long-term planning and investment decisions
- Creates "menu costs" — businesses must frequently update prices

**Trade-offs**
- Moderate inflation (2-3%) is generally considered healthy — allows wage flexibility
- Deflation is often worse — discourages spending, increases real debt burden

---

### 22.5 — Indexing and Its Limitations

**What is Indexing?**
- Automatic adjustment of wages, contracts, tax brackets, and benefits to account for inflation
- Examples: Social Security COLA (Cost of Living Adjustment), inflation-indexed bonds (TIPS)

**Benefits**
- Protects purchasing power without requiring new legislation
- Reduces political conflict over wage/benefit adjustments

**Limitations**
- Can perpetuate inflation (wage-price spiral)
- Based on CPI which has measurement biases
- Does not address root causes of inflation

## Chapter 28: Monetary Policy and Bank Regulation

### 28.1 — The Federal Reserve Banking System and Central Banks

**Structure of the Federal Reserve**
- **Board of Governors**: 7 members, 14-year staggered terms, appointed by President
- **12 Regional Federal Reserve Banks**: serve different geographic districts
- **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**: sets monetary policy, meets 8 times/year
- Semi-decentralized structure balances political independence with democratic accountability

**Functions of the Fed**
- Conducts monetary policy (manages money supply and interest rates)
- Supervises and regulates banks
- Maintains financial system stability
- Provides banking services (check clearing, electronic payments)

---

### 28.2 — Bank Regulation

**Why Regulate Banks?**
- Banks hold public deposits — failure affects millions
- Fractional reserve banking creates inherent vulnerability to bank runs
- Systemic risk — one bank's failure can cascade through the financial system

**Regulatory Tools**
| Tool | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Reserve requirements | Minimum cash banks must hold against deposits |
| Capital requirements | Minimum equity (owner's money) relative to assets |
| Deposit insurance (FDIC) | Protects depositors up to $250,000 per account |
| Bank examinations | Regular audits of bank health and practices |
| Lender of last resort | Fed provides emergency loans to prevent bank runs |

---

### 28.3 — How a Central Bank Executes Monetary Policy

**Primary Tools**

1. **Open Market Operations (OMO)** — most frequently used
   - **Expansionary**: Fed buys government bonds → injects money into banking system → increases money supply → lowers interest rates
   - **Contractionary**: Fed sells government bonds → removes money from banking system → decreases money supply → raises interest rates

2. **Reserve Requirements**
   - Raising reserve ratio → banks lend less → money supply contracts
   - Lowering reserve ratio → banks lend more → money supply expands
   - Rarely changed — too blunt an instrument

3. **Discount Rate**
   - Interest rate the Fed charges banks for short-term loans
   - Raising discount rate → borrowing more expensive → less lending
   - Lowering discount rate → borrowing cheaper → more lending

4. **Quantitative Easing (QE)** — unconventional tool
   - Used when interest rates are already near zero (zero lower bound)
   - Fed purchases long-term securities (mortgage-backed securities, long-term bonds)
   - Injects massive liquidity into the economy
   - Used extensively during 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19

---

### 28.4 — Monetary Policy and Economic Outcomes

**Expansionary Monetary Policy**
- Goal: stimulate economic activity during recession
- Mechanism: lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing → more investment and consumption → higher aggregate demand → higher output and employment
- Risk: can cause inflation if economy is already near full capacity

**Contractionary Monetary Policy**
- Goal: reduce inflation during economic overheating
- Mechanism: raise interest rates → more expensive borrowing → less investment and consumption → lower aggregate demand → lower inflation
- Risk: can cause recession and unemployment if applied too aggressively

---

### 28.5 — Pitfalls for Monetary Policy

| Challenge | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| Long and variable lags | 6-18 months for policy changes to affect the economy |
| Excess reserves | Banks may hold extra reserves rather than lending |
| Unstable velocity | Speed at which money circulates may change unpredictably |
| Zero lower bound | Cannot lower interest rates below zero (or very close to it) |
| Asset bubbles | Low rates can inflate stock/housing prices unsustainably |
| Political pressure | Demands for short-term stimulus may conflict with long-term stability |

## Chapter 30: Government Budgets and Fiscal Policy

### 30.1 — Government Spending

**Levels of Government Spending**
- **Federal**: defense, Social Security, Medicare/Medicaid, interest on debt
- **State**: education, highways, public welfare, corrections
- **Local**: K-12 education, police/fire, infrastructure, parks

**Federal Budget**
- Revenue sources: individual income tax (~50%), payroll taxes (~35%), corporate tax (~7%)
- Deficit = spending > revenue in a given year
- Surplus = revenue > spending in a given year
- National debt = accumulated deficits over time

---

### 30.2 — Taxation

**Types of Tax Structures**
| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Progressive | Higher income → higher tax rate | Federal income tax |
| Proportional (flat) | Same rate for all income levels | Some state income taxes |
| Regressive | Lower income → higher effective rate | Sales tax, payroll tax |

**Key Principle**: Tax structure affects both revenue collection and income distribution

---

### 30.3 — Federal Deficits and the National Debt

- Annual deficit adds to national debt
- Debt-to-GDP ratio is more meaningful than absolute debt level
- Economic growth reduces debt-to-GDP ratio even without paying down debt
- Recessions increase deficits (lower tax revenue + higher spending on safety nets)

---

### 30.4 — Using Fiscal Policy to Fight Recession, Unemployment, and Inflation

**Expansionary Fiscal Policy** (fight recession/unemployment)
- Increase government spending → directly increases aggregate demand
- Cut taxes → increases disposable income → consumers spend more
- Both shift AD curve to the right

**Contractionary Fiscal Policy** (fight inflation)
- Decrease government spending → directly reduces aggregate demand
- Raise taxes → reduces disposable income → consumers spend less
- Both shift AD curve to the left

**Multiplier Effect**
- Government spending has a multiplied effect on GDP
- $1 of spending → more than $1 of GDP increase (due to respending)
- Tax cuts have a smaller multiplier than direct spending (some tax savings are saved, not spent)

---

### 30.5 — Automatic Stabilizers

**Definition**: Government programs that automatically increase spending or reduce taxes during recessions without requiring new legislation

**Examples**
- **Unemployment insurance**: payments increase during recession as more workers lose jobs
- **Progressive income tax**: tax revenue falls automatically as incomes decline
- **Welfare/SNAP benefits**: enrollment increases during economic downturns

**How They Work**
- During recession: automatic stabilizers inject money into economy → cushion the downturn
- During expansion: stabilizers withdraw money → prevent overheating
- No legislative action required — built into existing law

**Standardized Employment Budget**
- Measures what the budget balance would be at full employment
- Separates automatic stabilizer effects from discretionary policy changes

---

### 30.6 — Practical Problems with Discretionary Fiscal Policy

| Problem | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| Recognition lag | Takes time to identify that a recession has begun |
| Legislative lag | Political process delays policy implementation |
| Implementation lag | Time for spending programs to actually deploy |
| Crowding out | Government borrowing raises interest rates → reduces private investment |
| Political constraints | Politicians prefer tax cuts and spending increases over austerity |
| Timing | Policy may take effect after the recession is already over |

---

### 30.7 — The Question of a Balanced Budget

**Arguments For Balanced Budget**
- Prevents unsustainable debt accumulation
- Forces fiscal discipline
- Reduces interest payments that crowd out productive spending

**Arguments Against Balanced Budget Requirement**
- Eliminates automatic stabilizers (must cut spending during recession)
- Prevents countercyclical fiscal policy
- Strategic investment (infrastructure, education) may justify temporary deficits

**Key Insight**: The debate is not about whether deficits are good or bad, but about when they are appropriate and what they fund

## Fiscal-Monetary Policy Interaction

**How They Work Together**
- Fiscal policy (government) and monetary policy (central bank) both influence aggregate demand
- Coordinated response: both expansionary during severe recession (e.g., COVID-19)
- Conflicting response: expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary → mixed signals

**COVID-19 Example**
- Fiscal: stimulus checks, expanded unemployment, PPP loans, tax deferrals
- Monetary: near-zero interest rates, massive QE, emergency lending facilities
- Result: prevented deep depression but contributed to post-pandemic inflation
