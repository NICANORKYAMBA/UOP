## Chapter 17: Financial Markets

### 17.1 — How Businesses Raise Financial Capital

**Sources of Capital for Firms**

| Source | Description | Trade-offs |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Early-stage investors (Angels/VC) | Equity in exchange for capital + mentorship | High dilution, loss of control, but no repayment obligation |
| Bank loans | Borrowed funds with interest | Must repay regardless of profit; requires collateral |
| Bonds (corporate) | Debt securities sold to investors | Fixed interest payments; lower cost than equity but increases leverage |
| Stock (IPO) | Selling ownership shares publicly | Maximum capital access; permanent dilution; regulatory burden |
| Retained earnings | Reinvesting profits | Cheapest source (no interest, no dilution); limits current distributions |

**Key Concepts:**
- Firms choose capital sources based on **risk tolerance**, **growth stage**, and **control preferences**
- Startups without revenue history cannot access debt markets → must use equity
- Mature firms prefer debt (tax-deductible interest) over equity (permanent dilution)
- Trade-off: debt increases financial risk (must repay) but preserves ownership; equity eliminates repayment risk but dilutes control

### 17.2 — How Households Supply Financial Capital

**Household Investment Options:**

| Asset | Return | Risk | Liquidity |
|-------|--------|------|-----------|
| Bank savings accounts | Low (1-3%) | Very low (FDIC insured) | Very high |
| Certificates of deposit | Moderate (3-5%) | Low | Low (penalty for early withdrawal) |
| Bonds | Moderate (4-7%) | Low-moderate | Moderate |
| Stocks | High (8-12% historical avg) | High (volatile) | High (publicly traded) |
| Mutual funds | Moderate-high | Moderate (diversified) | High |
| Real estate | Moderate-high | Moderate | Very low |

**Key principle:** Higher expected returns require accepting higher risk — no free lunch in financial markets.

### 17.3 — How to Accumulate Personal Wealth

- **Random walk theory**: Stock prices move unpredictably; consistently beating the market is nearly impossible
- **Compound interest**: Earning interest on previously earned interest — exponential growth over time
  - Formula: A = P(1 + r)^n
  - $1,000 at 7% for 30 years = $7,612 (vs $3,100 with simple interest)
- **Key insight**: Time in the market matters more than timing the market

## Chapter 27: Money and Banking

### 27.1 — Defining Money by Its Functions

| Function | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Medium of exchange | Accepted for buying/selling goods | Paying for groceries with cash |
| Store of value | Retains purchasing power over time | Saving money for future use |
| Unit of account | Standard measure for pricing | All goods priced in KES or USD |
| Standard of deferred payment | Basis for credit and debt contracts | Loan repayment in future dollars |

**Commodity money** — has intrinsic value (gold, silver)
**Fiat money** — value from government decree and public trust (modern currencies)

### 27.2 — Measuring Money: M1 and M2

| Measure | Components | Liquidity |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| M1 | Currency + checkable deposits + traveler's checks | Highest — immediately spendable |
| M2 | M1 + savings deposits + time deposits < $100K + money market funds | Lower — requires conversion |

### 27.3 — The Role of Banks

- Banks are **financial intermediaries** — connect savers (depositors) with borrowers (loan recipients)
- Banks reduce **transaction costs** by pooling deposits and screening borrowers
- Banks profit from the **interest rate spread** — charge borrowers more than they pay depositors
- **Bank failure** occurs when loan defaults exceed the bank's capital reserves (asset-liability mismatch)

### 27.4 — How Banks Create Money

**Fractional Reserve Banking:**
- Banks hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves; lend the rest
- Each loan creates a new deposit → which enables another loan → money multiplier effect

**Money Multiplier Formula:**
```
Money Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Ratio
```

**Example:** If reserve ratio = 10%:
- Multiplier = 1/0.10 = 10
- $1,000 initial deposit → up to $10,000 total money supply

**T-Account Example:**
```
Bank A receives $1,000 deposit
  Reserves: $100 (10%)
  Loans out: $900

Bank B receives $900 deposit (from Bank A's borrower spending)
  Reserves: $90
  Loans out: $810

Bank C receives $810 deposit...
  (continues until total deposits = $10,000)
```

**Key insight:** Banks don't lend out existing money — they CREATE new money through lending. The reserve ratio limits how much can be created.

## Chapter 29: Exchange Rates and International Capital Flows

### 29.1 — How the Foreign Exchange Market Works

- **Exchange rate** = price of one currency in terms of another
- **Appreciation** = currency becomes stronger (buys more foreign currency)
- **Depreciation** = currency becomes weaker (buys less foreign currency)

**Types of international investment:**
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) | Buying/building productive assets abroad (factories, companies) |
| Portfolio investment | Buying foreign stocks, bonds, financial assets |
| Hedging | Using financial instruments to protect against exchange rate risk |

### 29.2 — Demand and Supply Shifts in Foreign Exchange Markets

**Factors that shift demand/supply for a currency:**
- Interest rate differentials (higher rates attract foreign capital → currency appreciates)
- Inflation differentials (higher inflation → currency depreciates)
- Economic growth expectations
- Political stability
- Speculation and market sentiment

**Purchasing Power Parity (PPP):** In the long run, exchange rates adjust so that identical goods cost the same in different countries when converted to a common currency.

### 29.3 — Macroeconomic Effects of Exchange Rates

- **Strong currency**: cheaper imports, more expensive exports → trade deficit widens
- **Weak currency**: expensive imports, cheaper exports → trade deficit narrows
- Exchange rate volatility disrupts international lending and banking stability
- Sudden depreciation can trigger debt crises (foreign-denominated loans become more expensive)

### 29.4 — Exchange Rate Policies

| Policy | Description | Trade-offs |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Floating rate | Market determines value | Full monetary autonomy; high volatility |
| Soft peg | Central bank intervenes to keep rate within a band | Some stability; requires reserves; limited autonomy |
| Hard peg | Fixed rate maintained by law/currency board | Maximum stability; zero monetary autonomy |
| Merged currency | Adopt another country's currency (dollarization) or join currency union (Euro) | Eliminates exchange risk entirely; complete loss of independent monetary policy |

**Impossible Trinity:** A country cannot simultaneously have:
1. Fixed exchange rate
2. Free capital flows
3. Independent monetary policy

Must sacrifice one of the three.

## Digital Finance and Cryptocurrencies

### Key Concepts

**Cryptocurrency characteristics:**
- Decentralized (no central authority)
- Blockchain-based (distributed ledger technology)
- Pseudonymous transactions
- Limited supply (Bitcoin capped at 21 million)
- Borderless transfers without intermediaries

**Benefits:**
- Lower transaction costs (especially cross-border)
- Financial inclusion for unbanked populations
- Censorship resistance
- Programmable money (smart contracts)

**Challenges:**
- Price volatility (Bitcoin can swing 10-20% in days)
- Regulatory uncertainty across jurisdictions
- Energy consumption (proof-of-work mining)
- Limited merchant acceptance
- Potential for illicit use

**Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs):**
- Government-issued digital currencies
- Combine benefits of digital payments with central bank backing
- Maintain monetary policy control unlike decentralized crypto
- Examples: China's Digital Yuan, Nigeria's eNaira
