## 1. Core Concepts: Externalities and Market Failure (Section 12.1)

An **externality** exists when production or consumption imposes costs or benefits on third parties outside the market transaction. Because these spillover effects are not fully priced, markets produce inefficient outcomes.

### 1.1 Negative Externalities
A **negative externality** occurs when private activity imposes social costs not borne by producers or consumers directly involved in exchange.

Examples:
- Factory emissions affecting respiratory health in nearby communities
- Traffic congestion and air pollution from urban transport
- Noise pollution around airports and industrial zones

Economic implication:
- Firms base decisions on **private marginal cost (PMC)**, not **social marginal cost (SMC)**
- Since $SMC > PMC$, market output is too high and price is too low relative to the social optimum

### 1.2 Positive Externalities
A **positive externality** occurs when private actions create external benefits for others.

Examples:
- Vaccination reducing disease spread
- Education increasing productivity and civic outcomes
- Urban tree planting improving local climate and air quality

Economic implication:
- Markets consider **private marginal benefit (PMB)**, not full **social marginal benefit (SMB)**
- Since $SMB > PMB$, market output is too low relative to socially optimal output

### 1.3 Why This Is Market Failure
In both cases, equilibrium does not reflect total social costs/benefits. Resource allocation becomes inefficient (deadweight loss), creating justification for policy intervention.

## 2. Command-and-Control Regulation (Section 12.2)

**Command-and-control** policies use direct legal requirements:
- Emission standards
- Technology mandates (e.g., scrubbers)
- Bans or limits on pollutants

### Strengths
- Can quickly reduce severe pollution
- Works when pollution thresholds are non-negotiable (e.g., toxins)
- Easy to communicate politically: "do not exceed this level"

### Limitations
- Inflexible across firms with different abatement costs
- Weak incentives to innovate beyond compliance
- Enforcement and monitoring can be expensive

Policy insight: command-and-control is often effective for baseline protection but not always cost-minimizing.

## 3. Market-Oriented Environmental Tools (Section 12.3)

Market-based tools internalize external costs through price signals and incentives.

### 3.1 Pollution Charges (Pigouvian Taxes)
A tax per unit of emissions raises polluting activity cost.

Mechanism:
- Firms face higher marginal cost of polluting
- They reduce emissions where abatement is cheaper than paying tax

Advantage:
- Cost-effective reduction across heterogeneous firms

### 3.2 Marketable Permits (Cap-and-Trade)
Government sets a total emissions cap and allocates/sells permits.

Mechanism:
- Firms can trade permits
- Reductions occur where marginal abatement costs are lowest

Advantage:
- Environmental certainty (cap) plus market efficiency (trade)

### 3.3 Property Rights and Coasean Bargaining
If property rights are clear and transaction costs are low, affected parties can negotiate efficient outcomes.

Limitation in practice:
- Transaction costs, information asymmetry, and power imbalances often prevent full bargaining solutions, especially for global pollutants.

## 4. Benefits and Costs of Environmental Protection (Section 12.4)

Environmental policy is an economic choice under scarcity.

### 4.1 Benefit Channels
- Lower health expenditure
- Higher labor productivity
- Ecosystem service preservation
- Tourism and quality-of-life gains

### 4.2 Cost Channels
- Compliance and monitoring costs
- Possible short-run industry adjustment costs
- Transitional unemployment in pollution-intensive sectors

### 4.3 Marginal Analysis Rule
Optimal policy intensity is where:
- **Marginal Benefit of Pollution Reduction = Marginal Cost of Reduction**

Too little regulation leaves preventable social harm; too much can impose high compliance costs with small additional benefit.

## 5. International Environmental Issues (Section 12.5)

Global externalities (e.g., climate change, biodiversity loss) cross borders, creating collective-action problems.

### 5.1 Global Coordination Challenge
- Benefits of mitigation are global
- Costs are local and immediate
- Incentive to free-ride is high

### 5.2 High-Income vs. Low-Income Country Tensions
- High-income countries historically emitted more
- Low-income countries prioritize growth and poverty reduction
- Fair burden-sharing requires finance, technology transfer, and differentiated responsibilities

### 5.3 Paris Agreement Logic
The Paris framework combines global targets with decentralized national commitments, reflecting political and economic feasibility constraints.

## 6. Trade-Offs: Economic Output vs Environmental Protection (Section 12.6)

The **Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)** illustrates opportunity costs between market output and environmental quality.

### 6.1 Key Insight
Improving environmental quality may reduce output in the short run if resources are reallocated toward cleaner technology or compliance.

### 6.2 Dynamic Perspective
Well-designed market-oriented policies can shift the PPF outward over time by encouraging innovation (clean technology, energy efficiency), reducing the long-run trade-off severity.

## 7. Public Goods in Unit 3 Context

A **public good** is:
- **Non-rivalrous**: one person's consumption does not reduce others' consumption
- **Non-excludable**: difficult or impossible to exclude non-payers

Clean air is a near-classic public good in this framework.

### Provision Problem
Because of free-riding, private markets underprovide public goods. Government financing, regulation, and collective institutions are required for adequate provision.

---

## 8. Government Externalities (Trantidis, 2023)

Trantidis extends externality analysis to government action itself.

Core argument:
- Government decisions can impose costs on people outside the political decision network
- Clientelism and insider bargaining can generate "government externalities" similar to market failures

Policy implication:
- Intervention is necessary but not automatically efficient
- Institutional design, accountability, and transparency determine policy quality

---

## 9. Unit 3 Exam-Ready Distinctions

1. **Command-and-control vs market-based tools**:
Command-and-control sets quantity/technology rules directly; market-based tools use incentives and price signals.

2. **Negative vs positive externalities**:
Negative externalities imply overproduction; positive externalities imply underproduction.

3. **Public goods vs externalities**:
Externalities are spillovers in private activity; public goods are consumption/production problems tied to non-rivalry and non-excludability.

4. **Local vs global effects**:
Local externalities affect nearby communities; global externalities spread across national borders and require collective governance.
