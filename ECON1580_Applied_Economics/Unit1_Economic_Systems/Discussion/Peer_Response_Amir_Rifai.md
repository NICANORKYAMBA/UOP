# Peer Response — Amir Rifai (ECON 1580 Unit 1 Discussion)

Hi Amir,

Your post makes a strong case for public-private interdependence and your use of all three course readings as evidence gives it solid grounding. The point you raise about geopolitics and supply chain coordination is particularly relevant right now — Edmond (2024) captures something that most introductory economics discussions overlook, which is that the relationship between government and business is not just domestic but increasingly shaped by international political pressures that neither sector can navigate alone.

I want to build on your PPP example by adding a dimension you touched on but did not fully develop — the risk-sharing mechanism. The World Bank (2024) explains that what makes Hybrid PPPs effective is not just that government and private firms contribute different resources, but that they deliberately allocate financial risk to whichever party is better positioned to absorb it. Governments absorb demand risk and political risk through concessional financing, while private firms absorb operational and efficiency risk. This structured risk allocation is what makes private capital willing to enter markets it would otherwise avoid entirely. Without that design, the partnership collapses — either the government bears all the cost, or the private sector demands returns too high for the project to be viable for consumers.

Your conclusion that the relationship between the two sectors is one of mutual dependence rather than competition is well-stated and connects directly to what Greenlaw et al. (2022) describe in the circular flow model — government, households, businesses, and the foreign sector are all nodes in a single interconnected system, where each sector's decisions create ripple effects across the others (Section 1.3). A policy decision by government changes the incentive structure for businesses, which changes employment and income for households, which changes consumer demand, which feeds back into business investment decisions.

One question worth considering: in cases where government intervention is poorly designed — for example, subsidies that protect inefficient industries — does the interdependence you describe become a liability rather than a strength? That tension seems important for evaluating when public-private collaboration actually improves economic outcomes versus when it distorts them.

Well-argued post overall.
