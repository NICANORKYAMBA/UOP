Hi Pride,

This is the most analytically complete post I have read in this discussion. The Kribi Deep Seaport example is specific, well-evidenced, and — crucially — you do not stop at describing the partnership. You interrogate it, which is exactly what applied economics requires. The acknowledgment of environmental concerns and distributional equity in your final substantive paragraph elevates the analysis beyond a straightforward endorsement of PPPs and reflects the kind of critical thinking that Greenlaw et al. (2022) associate with sound economic reasoning — recognizing that efficiency and fairness are both legitimate objectives that mixed economies must balance simultaneously (Section 1.4).

The connection you draw to Ruzicka (2024) is particularly well-placed. The argument that supply chain diversification and strategic infrastructure investment build long-term economic resilience maps directly onto what the Kribi port achieves for Cameroon — reducing dependence on landlocked trade routes and positioning the country as a Central African maritime hub. This is not just a logistics improvement; it is a structural shift in Cameroon's economic geography that changes its bargaining position in regional and global trade networks. That is a macro-level consequence worth emphasizing more explicitly, because it connects your local example to the broader course theme of how global economic policies shape national development trajectories.

One dimension worth adding to your analysis is the role of sovereign guarantees in the Kribi PPP. When the Cameroonian government provided sovereign guarantees to the private consortium, it effectively transferred a portion of the country's fiscal risk onto future taxpayers. The World Bank (2024) notes that this is a standard feature of Hybrid PPPs, but it also means that if the project underperforms commercially, the public sector absorbs the loss. That contingent liability is a real cost of the interdependence you describe — and it is one reason why the quality of government oversight and contract design matters as much as the partnership itself.

Excellent work — the Kribi case deserves the detailed treatment you gave it.
