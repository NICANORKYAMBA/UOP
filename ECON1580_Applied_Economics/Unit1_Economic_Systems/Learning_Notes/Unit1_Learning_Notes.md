## 1. What is Economics? (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 1.1)

Economics is the study of how individuals, businesses, and governments make decisions in the face of **scarcity** — the fundamental condition where resources are limited but human wants are unlimited. Every economic decision involves a **trade-off**: choosing one option means giving up another. The value of the next best alternative foregone is called the **opportunity cost**.

**Scarcity and Specialization**
- Adam Smith's **division of labor**: when workers specialize in specific tasks, productivity rises
- Specialization enables **economies of scale** — producing more at lower cost per unit
- Trade-off: specialization creates efficiency but also vulnerability to demand shifts and technological change

**Key economic questions every system must answer:**
1. What to produce?
2. How to produce it?
3. For whom to produce it?

## 2. Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics (Section 1.2)

| Feature | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| Focus | Individual decisions | Entire economy |
| Studies | Consumers, firms, markets | GDP, inflation, unemployment |
| Tools | Supply & demand, market structures | Fiscal policy, monetary policy |
| Example | How a firm sets its price | How a recession affects employment |

Both are connected — micro decisions aggregate into macro outcomes. A recession (macro) reduces consumer spending, which affects individual businesses (micro).

**Policy tools at the macro level:**
- **Fiscal policy** — government spending and taxation (managed by government)
- **Monetary policy** — money supply and interest rates (managed by central banks)

## 3. Economic Models and the Circular Flow (Section 1.3)

Economists use **theories and models** to simplify complex reality. A model is not meant to be perfectly realistic — it is meant to isolate key relationships.

**The Circular Flow Diagram** — four sectors:

| Sector | Role |
|--------|------|
| Households | Provide labor; consume goods and services |
| Businesses | Produce goods and services; hire labor |
| Government | Collects taxes; provides public services |
| Foreign Sector | Facilitates international trade; affects capital flow |

Money flows clockwise (payments for goods, wages, taxes), resources flow counterclockwise (labor, goods, services).

## 4. Types of Economic Systems (Section 1.4)

| System | Who decides? | Key feature | Strength | Weakness |
|--------|-------------|-------------|----------|----------|
| Traditional | Custom/tradition | Based on habits and culture | Stability, continuity | Resists innovation |
| Command | Central government | State controls all production | Rapid industrialization | Inefficiency, no consumer choice |
| Market | Supply & demand | Prices set by competition | Innovation, efficiency | Income inequality |
| Mixed | Both government + market | Combines market freedom with regulation | Balances growth and social protection | Difficult to calibrate |

Most countries today operate **mixed economies** — the U.S., Kenya, Germany, China all blend market mechanisms with government intervention to varying degrees.

**Globalization** has deepened trade and interconnectedness but faces resistance due to concerns about job displacement and rising inequality (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 1.4).

## 5. Public-Private Sector Interdependence

**Public sector** — government bodies that provide public goods, regulate markets, collect taxes, and implement policy.

**Private sector** — businesses and individuals that produce goods and services for profit in competitive markets.

In a mixed economy, neither sector operates in isolation:
- Government creates the **regulatory framework** within which private firms operate
- Private firms generate **tax revenue** that funds public services
- Public investment in infrastructure, education, and R&D **reduces costs** for private firms
- Private sector **efficiency and innovation** deliver outcomes government cannot achieve alone

**Hybrid Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs)** combine government funding, private sector efficiency, and development finance to deliver infrastructure. The World Bank (2024) identifies PPPs as particularly effective in low-income regions where governments lack capital but need infrastructure investment. By sharing financial risk between public and private partners, PPPs attract investment while maintaining affordability and public accountability.

## 6. Geopolitics and Trade Policy (Edmond, 2024)

Geopolitical tensions are increasingly shaping trade policy. Nations are prioritizing:
- **Security** over pure economic efficiency (e.g., reshoring critical supply chains)
- **Friendshoring** — trading preferentially with allied nations
- **Reduced dependencies** on single-country suppliers (e.g., semiconductors, rare earths)

This fragmentation risks undermining the efficiency gains from globalization. Ruzicka (2024) argues that balanced approaches — supply chain diversification, energy independence, and multilateral cooperation — are needed to maintain long-term stability.
