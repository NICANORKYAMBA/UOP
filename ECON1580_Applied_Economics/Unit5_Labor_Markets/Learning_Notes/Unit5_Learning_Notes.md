## Chapter 14: Labor Markets and Income

### 14.1 — The Theory of Labor Markets

**Demand for Labor**
- Labor demand is a **derived demand** — firms hire workers not for their own sake but because workers produce goods/services that can be sold
- **Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)** = additional revenue from hiring one more worker
  - Formula: MRP = Marginal Product × Price
  - Firms hire workers up to the point where MRP = wage
- In **perfectly competitive** output markets, firms are price takers
- In **imperfectly competitive** output markets (monopoly, oligopoly), firms face downward-sloping demand curves

**Labor Supply**
- Individual labor supply curve typically slopes upward — higher wages attract more workers
- **Labor-leisure trade-off**: workers must choose between earning income (labor) and free time (leisure)
- Market labor supply = sum of all individual labor supply curves

**Equilibrium Wage and Employment**
- Equilibrium occurs where labor supply = labor demand
- Competitive equilibrium: wage = MRP = marginal cost of labor

---

### 14.2 — Wages and Employment in Imperfectly Competitive Labor Markets

**Monopsony Power**
- **Monopsony** = single buyer of labor (e.g., company town with one major employer)
- Monopsonist faces **upward-sloping labor supply curve** — must raise wages to attract more workers
- **Marginal factor cost (MFC)** > wage because raising wages for one worker means raising them for all workers
- Monopsonist hires where MRP = MFC, resulting in:
  - **Lower wages** than competitive market
  - **Fewer workers** hired than competitive market

**U.S. Workplace Protection Laws**
- **Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)**: minimum wage, overtime pay, child labor restrictions
- **Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA)**: workplace safety standards
- **Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)**: unpaid leave for family/medical reasons
- These laws aim to balance employer power with worker protections

---

### 14.3 — Market Power on the Supply Side: Unions

**Role of Labor Unions**
- **Collective bargaining**: unions negotiate wages, benefits, working conditions on behalf of members
- Unions can raise wages above competitive equilibrium by restricting labor supply
- **Union wage premium**: union workers typically earn 10-20% more than non-union workers in similar jobs

**Benefits of Unionization**
- Higher wages and better benefits
- Improved workplace safety
- Job security and grievance procedures
- Voice in workplace decisions

**Drawbacks of Unionization**
- Reduced employment (higher wages → firms hire fewer workers)
- Potential inefficiency (rigid work rules)
- Conflict between union and non-union workers

**Decline in U.S. Union Membership**
- Peak: 35% of workforce in 1950s
- Current: ~10% of workforce
- Causes:
  - Shift from manufacturing to service economy
  - Globalization and outsourcing
  - Right-to-work laws (workers can opt out of union membership)
  - Cultural shift toward individualism

---

### 14.4 — Bilateral Monopoly

**Definition**
- **Bilateral monopoly** = monopsony employer meets monopoly union
- Union has monopoly power over labor supply
- Employer has monopsony power over labor demand

**Outcomes**
- Employment falls **below** competitive level
- Wage is **indeterminate** — lies between union's preferred wage (high) and monopsonist's preferred wage (low)
- Actual wage determined by bargaining power and negotiation

---

### 14.5 — Employment Discrimination

**Earnings Gaps**
- **Gender pay gap**: women earn ~80-85% of what men earn for similar work
- **Racial pay gap**: Black and Hispanic workers earn less than white workers with similar education/experience

**Causes of Discrimination**
- **Taste-based discrimination**: employer prejudice against certain groups
- **Statistical discrimination**: employers use group averages (stereotypes) to make hiring decisions
- **Occupational segregation**: women and minorities concentrated in lower-paying occupations
- **Educational disparities**: unequal access to quality education

**U.S. Anti-Discrimination Policies**
- **Equal Pay Act (1963)**: requires equal pay for equal work
- **Civil Rights Act Title VII (1964)**: prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin
- **Affirmative action**: policies to increase representation of underrepresented groups

**Economic Impact**
- Discrimination reduces economic efficiency — talent is wasted
- Persistent earnings gaps undermine economic growth

---

### 14.6 — Immigration

**Historical Patterns**
- Early 1900s: European immigrants (Italy, Ireland, Germany)
- Post-1965: shift to Latin America, Asia (Mexico, China, India, Philippines)
- Current: ~14% of U.S. population is foreign-born

**Economic Impacts**
- **Wages**: mixed evidence
  - High-skilled immigrants may raise wages for complementary workers
  - Low-skilled immigrants may lower wages for competing workers (small effect, ~1-3%)
- **Employment**: immigrants create jobs by starting businesses and increasing demand
- **Public budgets**: immigrants pay taxes but also use public services
  - Net fiscal impact depends on skill level and time horizon

**Reform Proposals**
- **Points-based system**: prioritize high-skilled immigrants (used in Canada, Australia)
- **Guest worker programs**: temporary visas for seasonal workers
- **Pathway to citizenship**: legalize undocumented immigrants
- **Border enforcement**: reduce illegal immigration

## Chapter 15: Poverty and Economic Inequality

### 15.1 — Drawing the Poverty Line

**U.S. Poverty Line**
- Based on **cost of nutritionally adequate diet** × 3 (assumes food = 1/3 of budget)
- 2023: ~$14,000 for individual, ~$29,000 for family of four
- Adjusted annually for inflation

**Poverty Rate Trends**
- 1960s: ~22% (before War on Poverty)
- 1970s-2000s: fluctuates between 11-15%
- 2020: spike due to COVID-19, then decline due to stimulus payments
- **Demographic disparities**:
  - Children: higher poverty rate than adults
  - Single-parent households: much higher poverty rate
  - Racial minorities: higher poverty rates than whites

**International Comparisons**
- U.S. poverty rate higher than most developed countries
- Different countries use different poverty measures (absolute vs. relative)

---

### 15.2 — The Poverty Trap

**Definition**
- **Poverty trap**: situation where government assistance creates disincentive to work
- As earnings increase, benefits decrease dollar-for-dollar → **effective marginal tax rate near 100%**

**Example**
- Worker earning $10,000 receives $5,000 in benefits → total income $15,000
- If worker earns $15,000, benefits drop to $0 → total income still $15,000
- No financial gain from working more

**Policy Solutions**
- **Gradual benefit reduction**: reduce benefits by $0.50 for every $1 earned (instead of $1-for-$1)
- **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**: supplements earnings for low-income workers
- **Time limits**: require work after receiving benefits for certain period

---

### 15.3 — The Safety Net

**Major U.S. Programs**
- **TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families)**: cash assistance, work requirements, time limits
- **SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program)**: food assistance (formerly food stamps)
- **Medicaid**: health insurance for low-income individuals
- **EITC (Earned Income Tax Credit)**: refundable tax credit for working families
- **Housing assistance**: Section 8 vouchers, public housing

**Program Changes Over Time**
- 1996 welfare reform: added work requirements, time limits to TANF
- EITC expansion: now largest anti-poverty program for working families
- Medicaid expansion under ACA (2014): extended coverage to more low-income adults

**Challenges**
- **Work requirements**: may push people into low-wage jobs without addressing root causes
- **Benefit cliffs**: sudden loss of benefits when income crosses threshold
- **Program costs**: trade-off between generosity and fiscal sustainability

---

### 15.4 — Income Inequality: Measurement and Causes

**Measuring Inequality**
- **Quintiles**: divide population into five equal groups by income
  - Top 20% earn ~50% of total income
  - Bottom 20% earn ~3% of total income
- **Lorenz curve**: plots cumulative income share against cumulative population share
  - Perfect equality = 45-degree line
  - Greater inequality = curve bows further from line
- **Gini coefficient**: area between Lorenz curve and 45-degree line
  - 0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality
  - U.S. Gini coefficient: ~0.48 (high among developed countries)

**Trends in U.S. Inequality**
- 1950s-1970s: relatively stable, moderate inequality
- 1980s-present: **rising inequality**
  - Top 1% income share doubled (from ~10% to ~20%)
  - Middle-class income growth stagnated
  - CEO-to-worker pay ratio increased from 20:1 to 300:1

**Causes of Rising Inequality**
- **Technology**: skill-biased technological change favors high-skilled workers
- **Globalization**: outsourcing reduces demand for low-skilled workers in developed countries
- **Decline of unions**: reduced bargaining power for workers
- **Education premium**: college graduates earn much more than high school graduates
- **Tax policy**: lower top marginal tax rates since 1980s
- **Winner-take-all markets**: superstars capture disproportionate share of income

**Poverty vs. Inequality**
- **Poverty** = absolute standard (can everyone meet basic needs?)
- **Inequality** = relative standard (how is income distributed?)
- Possible to reduce poverty without reducing inequality (raise all incomes)
- Possible to reduce inequality without reducing poverty (redistribute without growth)

---

### 15.5 — Government Policies to Reduce Inequality

**Redistribution Policies**
- **Progressive taxation**: higher earners pay higher tax rates
  - U.S. federal income tax: 10% to 37% marginal rates
  - Capital gains taxed at lower rates (15-20%)
- **Transfer payments**: Social Security, unemployment insurance, welfare
- **In-kind transfers**: Medicaid, SNAP, housing assistance

**Inheritance Taxes**
- **Estate tax**: tax on wealth transferred at death
  - Current: 40% rate on estates over $13 million (2024)
  - Debate: does it reduce inequality or discourage saving/investment?

**Equal Opportunity Policies**
- **Public education**: free K-12 education, subsidized higher education
- **Head Start**: early childhood education for low-income families
- **Job training programs**: help workers gain skills for better-paying jobs
- **Anti-discrimination laws**: ensure equal access to employment

**Trade-offs**
- **Equity vs. efficiency**: redistribution may reduce work incentives
- **Leaky bucket**: some resources lost in transfer process (administrative costs)
- **Political feasibility**: redistribution faces opposition from high earners

## Chapter 6: Consumer Choices

### 6.1 — Consumption Choices

**Utility Maximization**
- **Utility** = satisfaction from consuming goods/services
- **Total utility** = total satisfaction from all units consumed
- **Marginal utility** = additional satisfaction from consuming one more unit
- **Diminishing marginal utility**: each additional unit provides less satisfaction than the previous one

**Optimal Consumption Rule**
- Consumers maximize utility by allocating budget so that:
  - **MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂ = ... = MUₙ/Pₙ**
  - Marginal utility per dollar is equal across all goods

**Example: José's T-shirts and Movies**
- José has $56 budget
- T-shirts cost $14, movies cost $7
- Optimal: 2 T-shirts, 4 movies (MU per dollar equal at 4 utils/$)

---

### 6.2 — How Income and Prices Affect Consumption

**Budget Constraint**
- Shows all combinations of goods consumer can afford
- Slope = -P₁/P₂ (price ratio)
- Changes in income → parallel shift
- Changes in price → rotation

**Income Effect**
- Higher income → can afford more of all goods (if normal goods)
- Lower income → must consume less

**Substitution Effect**
- Price increase → good becomes relatively more expensive → substitute toward other goods
- Price decrease → good becomes relatively cheaper → substitute away from other goods

**Demand Curve Derivation**
- As price falls, quantity demanded rises due to:
  - Substitution effect (good is relatively cheaper)
  - Income effect (real income increases)

---

### 6.3 — Behavioral Economics

**Deviations from Rational Choice**
- Traditional economics assumes rational, utility-maximizing behavior
- Behavioral economics recognizes psychological biases

**Key Concepts**
- **Loss aversion**: losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good
  - People reject 50-50 bet to win $110 or lose $100 (even though expected value is positive)
- **Mental accounting**: people treat money differently depending on its source or intended use
  - More likely to spend tax refund than regular paycheck
- **Overconfidence**: people overestimate their abilities and knowledge
  - 80% of drivers think they're above average
- **Present bias**: people overvalue immediate rewards relative to future rewards
  - Explains low savings rates, procrastination
- **Framing effects**: decisions depend on how choices are presented
  - "90% lean" vs. "10% fat" ground beef

**Intertemporal Choices**
- Decisions involving trade-offs over time (save now vs. spend now)
- Hyperbolic discounting: people are impatient in short run, patient in long run
  - Prefer $100 today over $110 tomorrow
  - But prefer $110 in 31 days over $100 in 30 days

## Supplementary Readings

### Handel (2024) — Labor Shortages

**Definition and Measurement**
- **Labor shortage** = sustained excess demand for workers at prevailing wages
- Hard to measure directly — use proxies:
  - Job vacancy rates
  - Time to fill positions
  - Wage growth
  - Quit rates

**Drivers of Labor Shortages**
- **Demographic changes**: aging population, declining birth rates
- **Skill mismatches**: workers lack skills employers need
- **Immigration restrictions**: reduce labor supply
- **Technological change**: rapid shifts in skill requirements
- **COVID-19 impacts**: early retirements, career changes, health concerns

**Are All Shortages Problems?**
- Some shortages signal healthy labor market (workers have bargaining power)
- Persistent shortages may indicate structural issues
- Wage increases can resolve shortages if labor supply is elastic

**Solutions**
- **Education and training**: upskill workers for in-demand jobs
- **Immigration reform**: attract high-skilled workers
- **Automation**: substitute capital for labor
- **Wage increases**: attract workers from other industries

### Ruhana et al. (2024) — Combating Poverty and Inequality

**Causes of Poverty and Inequality**
- **Economic factors**: unemployment, low wages, lack of capital
- **Environmental factors**: climate change, natural disasters
- **Policy factors**: regressive taxation, weak social safety nets
- **Structural factors**: discrimination, unequal access to education

**Solutions**
- **Global cooperation**: international aid, debt relief, technology transfer
- **Innovation**: new technologies to improve productivity
- **Inclusive development**: policies that benefit all groups, not just elites
- **Education and healthcare**: invest in human capital
- **Progressive taxation**: redistribute income from rich to poor

## Quick Study Guide

### Labor Market Equilibrium

| Market Type | Wage | Employment | Efficiency |
|-------------|------|------------|------------|
| Competitive | W* = MRP | L* (optimal) | Efficient |
| Monopsony | W < MRP | L < L* | Inefficient (too few workers) |
| Union | W > W* | L < L* | Inefficient (too few jobs) |
| Bilateral monopoly | Indeterminate | L < L* | Inefficient |

### Poverty vs. Inequality

| Concept | Definition | Measurement | Policy Goal |
|---------|------------|-------------|-------------|
| Poverty | Absolute deprivation | Poverty line, poverty rate | Ensure basic needs met |
| Inequality | Relative distribution | Gini coefficient, quintile shares | Reduce income gaps |

### Consumer Choice Rules

- **Utility maximization**: MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂
- **Diminishing marginal utility**: MU falls as consumption increases
- **Income effect**: higher income → more consumption (normal goods)
- **Substitution effect**: higher price → substitute to other goods

### Behavioral Economics Biases

- **Loss aversion**: losses > gains
- **Present bias**: now > later
- **Mental accounting**: money not fungible
- **Overconfidence**: overestimate abilities
- **Framing**: presentation matters
