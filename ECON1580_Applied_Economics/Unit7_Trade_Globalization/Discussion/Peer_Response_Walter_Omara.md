Hi Walter,

Your analysis of Uganda's globalization experience is well-structured and demonstrates a strong command of the AD/AS framework. The parallel between Kenya and Uganda is striking — both East African economies face the same fundamental tension between growth-enabling trade openness and vulnerability to imported inflation through global commodity price shocks. Your specific examples — MTN Uganda's mobile banking expansion, coffee exporters accessing AfCFTA markets, and the tourism sector recovery — ground the theoretical discussion in observable economic activity, which strengthens the analysis considerably.

I particularly appreciate your application of the AD/AS model to distinguish between positive globalization effects (rightward AD and AS shifts from FDI, exports, and technology transfer) and negative transmission effects (leftward SRAS shifts from imported fuel and fertilizer price increases). This dual-direction analysis captures the complexity that a simpler "globalization is good/bad" framing would miss. Greenlaw et al. (2022) emphasize that the same trade openness that enables growth also creates channels for external shock transmission — your Uganda case study illustrates this perfectly (Section 24.3).

One dimension I would encourage you to explore further is the relationship between Uganda's trade deficit and financial capital flows. Uganda, like Kenya, runs a persistent current account deficit financed by FDI and remittances. Greenlaw et al. (2022) explain that when a country imports more than it exports, the difference must be financed through capital inflows — either foreign investment or borrowing (Section 23.3). This creates a dependency: if global financial conditions tighten (as they did in 2022-2023 when the U.S. Federal Reserve raised rates), capital flows to emerging markets like Uganda can reverse suddenly, putting pressure on the exchange rate and forcing the Bank of Uganda into contractionary policy even when domestic conditions might warrant stimulus. This financial channel of globalization — beyond just trade in goods — is an important additional mechanism through which external conditions constrain domestic macroeconomic policy.

Your reference to the Bank of Uganda raising interest rates to control imported inflation is a clear policy example that connects directly to the AD/AS model. In terms of the three zones Greenlaw et al. (2022) describe — Keynesian, intermediate, and neoclassical — Uganda's policy response suggests the economy was operating in the intermediate zone where both output and price effects matter, making the contractionary choice a genuine trade-off rather than a straightforward decision (Section 24.6).

Strong post with excellent use of diverse, credible sources.
