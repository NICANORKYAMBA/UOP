## Part 1: Investments in Innovation (Section 13.1)

### 1.1 Private vs. Social Benefits of Innovation

When a firm invests in research and development (R&D), it creates two types of benefits:

- **Private benefit**: the profit the firm earns from its innovation — captured through sales, patents, or competitive advantage
- **Social benefit**: the broader benefit to society from the innovation — spillovers to other firms, industries, and consumers who benefit without paying

The **social benefit** of innovation almost always exceeds the **private benefit**. This gap is a **positive externality** — a benefit that flows to third parties who did not pay for it (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.1).

**Historical examples from the textbook:**
- The Wright Brothers' invention of the airplane created enormous social value — airlines, tourism, global trade — far beyond what they personally captured
- Inventors of the internet, GPS, and touchscreens created social benefits worth trillions, while capturing only a fraction as private profit

### 1.2 Why Firms Underinvest in R&D

Because firms cannot capture the full social benefit of their innovations, they invest **less** in R&D than is socially optimal. This is a **market failure**: the private market produces less innovation than society would benefit from.

The logic: if a firm spends $1 million on R&D but competitors can copy the innovation and capture half the market, the firm's private return may be only $400,000 — less than the cost. The firm will not invest. But the social return (including benefits to consumers and other industries) might be $3 million. Society loses $2.6 million in value because the market underproduces innovation (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.1).

## Part 2: How Governments Can Encourage Innovation (Section 13.2)

### 2.1 Intellectual Property Rights

**Patents** grant inventors the exclusive legal right to produce and sell their invention for a limited period (20 years in the US). This temporary monopoly allows the inventor to charge higher prices and recoup R&D investment, creating the private incentive to innovate.

**Copyrights** protect original creative works (books, music, software) for the life of the author plus 70 years.

**Trade-off**: Patents create temporary monopoly power, which raises prices and reduces access in the short run. But without patents, firms would not invest in innovation, and society would have fewer new products in the long run. The patent system is a deliberate policy choice to accept short-term monopoly costs in exchange for long-term innovation benefits (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.2).

### 2.2 Three US Government Policies for Innovation

Greenlaw et al. (2022, Section 13.2) identify three main policy approaches:

**1. Direct funding for R&D**
Government agencies (NIH, NSF, DARPA) directly fund basic research that private firms would not fund because the returns are too uncertain or too distant. The internet, GPS, and many pharmaceutical breakthroughs originated from government-funded research.

**2. Tax incentives for private R&D**
Tax credits reduce the after-tax cost of R&D investment, making it more attractive for private firms. The R&D tax credit in the US allows firms to deduct a percentage of R&D spending from their tax bill.

**3. Cooperative research**
Government agencies, universities, and private firms collaborate on research projects. Each contributes different resources: government provides funding and public interest perspective, universities provide basic research capacity, private firms provide applied expertise and commercialization capability.

## Part 3: Public Goods (Section 13.3)

### 3.1 Characteristics of Public Goods

A **public good** has two defining characteristics (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.3):

| Characteristic | Definition | Example |
|---------------|-----------|---------|
| **Non-excludable** | Cannot prevent non-payers from consuming it | National defense — cannot exclude citizens who don't pay taxes |
| **Non-rival** | One person's consumption does not reduce availability for others | A lighthouse — one ship using it doesn't prevent another |

**Examples of public goods**: national defense, clean air, public parks, basic scientific research, street lighting, flood control systems.

**Private goods** are both excludable and rival — a sandwich you eat cannot be eaten by someone else, and you can prevent others from taking it.

### 3.2 The Free Rider Problem

Because public goods are non-excludable, individuals have an incentive to **free ride** — to benefit from the good without paying for it. If everyone free rides, no one pays, and the good is not provided even though everyone would benefit from it. This is a market failure: the private market will underprovide public goods (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.3).

**Solution**: Government provision, funded by taxes. Since everyone benefits and no one can be excluded, collective funding through taxation is the only way to ensure the good is provided.

### 3.3 The Tragedy of the Commons

**Common resources** are non-excludable but rival — anyone can use them, but one person's use reduces availability for others. Examples: fisheries, groundwater, forests, clean air.

The **tragedy of the commons**: when a resource is shared and unregulated, each individual has an incentive to use as much as possible before others do. The result is overuse and depletion of the resource, even though everyone would be better off if use were limited (Greenlaw et al., 2022, Section 13.3).

**Policy solutions**:
- **Property rights**: assign ownership so the owner has an incentive to manage the resource sustainably
- **Regulations**: set limits on use (fishing quotas, emissions caps)
- **Taxes/fees**: charge users for the social cost of their consumption
- **Community management**: local communities develop their own rules for managing shared resources

## Part 4: Green Growth Strategy and Environmental Policy (Hutajulu et al., 2024)

Hutajulu et al. (2024) review four decades of research on green growth strategy, identifying key themes:

**Green growth** is an approach to economic development that simultaneously pursues economic growth and environmental sustainability — not as competing goals but as complementary ones. The core argument is that environmental degradation imposes long-term economic costs (resource depletion, health costs, climate damage) that outweigh the short-term gains from unregulated growth.

**Key policy instruments identified:**
- **Carbon pricing**: putting a price on carbon emissions to internalize the negative externality of greenhouse gas pollution
- **Green investment**: directing public and private capital toward renewable energy, energy efficiency, and sustainable infrastructure
- **Green jobs**: creating employment in clean energy, environmental services, and sustainable agriculture
- **Technological innovation**: investing in clean technologies that reduce the environmental cost of economic activity

The bibliometric analysis in Hutajulu et al. (2024) shows that research on green growth has grown exponentially since the 1990s, reflecting increasing recognition that environmental policy and economic policy cannot be designed independently.

## Part 5: Global Environmental Policies (Adanma & Ogunbiyi, 2024)

Adanma and Ogunbiyi (2024) compare environmental policies across different countries and regions, identifying three broad approaches:

**1. Command-and-control regulation**
Governments set mandatory standards — emission limits, technology requirements, land use restrictions. Firms must comply or face penalties. Advantage: certainty of outcome. Disadvantage: may not achieve compliance at lowest cost.

**2. Market-based instruments**
Carbon taxes, cap-and-trade systems, and tradeable permits use price signals to incentivize emission reductions. Firms that can reduce emissions cheaply do so; those for whom reduction is expensive pay others to reduce instead. Advantage: achieves environmental goals at lower total cost. Disadvantage: requires careful design to avoid loopholes.

**3. Voluntary agreements and corporate sustainability**
Businesses voluntarily commit to sustainability targets beyond legal requirements. Driven by consumer pressure, investor expectations (ESG criteria), and reputational considerations. Advantage: can move faster than regulation. Disadvantage: enforcement is weak and commitments may be superficial ("greenwashing").

**International cooperation**: Climate change is a global public good problem — no single country can solve it alone, and each country has an incentive to free ride on others' efforts. International agreements (Paris Agreement, Kyoto Protocol) attempt to coordinate action, but enforcement remains a challenge (Adanma & Ogunbiyi, 2024).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Positive externality | Benefit to third parties not reflected in market price |
| Negative externality | Cost to third parties not reflected in market price |
| Public good | Non-excludable and non-rival — market underprovides |
| Free rider problem | Benefiting from a public good without paying |
| Tragedy of the commons | Overuse of shared resources due to lack of property rights |
| Patent | Temporary exclusive right to produce an invention |
| Green growth | Economic development that simultaneously protects the environment |
| Carbon pricing | Charging emitters for the social cost of carbon emissions |
| Cap-and-trade | System setting total emission limit with tradeable permits |
| Command-and-control | Mandatory regulatory standards for environmental compliance |
